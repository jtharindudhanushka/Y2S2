Explainable AI and Ethics

Covers: the black-box problem, the goals of explainable AI (trust, debugging, fairness and auditing), the accuracy-interpretability trade-off, glass-box models, global vs local explanations, permutation importance, LIME and SHAP with worked numbers, the nine ethical foundations of using (Gen)AI, the EU AI Act (risk levels, duties, general-purpose AI, fines) and how to approach the EU case-analysis assignment. No prior lecture attendance needed.

Learning outcomes

  • Explain the black-box problem and why interpretability matters in high-stakes areas
  • State the three goals of XAI (trust, debugging, fairness and auditing) and recognise spurious correlations
  • Describe the accuracy-interpretability trade-off and the model families (glass-box, ensemble, deep learning)
  • Distinguish global from local explanations; explain and compute permutation importance, LIME and SHAP at an introductory level
  • List the nine ethical foundations of using (Gen)AI
  • Summarise the EU AI Act: four risk levels, duties of high-risk systems, general-purpose AI, fines; plan the case-analysis assignment

Sources: the lecturer's slides (Explainable AI: An Introduction, 12 slides, marked (slides)), the EU case-analysis assignment brief and the peer-made lesson (worked examples, EU AI Act summary, marked (peer notes)).

1. The black-box problem (slides)

  • Key problem: modern machine learning prioritises predictive performance over transparency. Complex algorithms achieve outstanding accuracy but hide their decision logic inside millions of parameters. A black box is a machine where you see what goes in and what comes out, but not what happens inside.
  • Why interpretability? In high-stakes applications unexplainable model failure poses critical risks: medical diagnosis (life safety), credit scoring (financial loss), autonomous driving (legal liability).
  • Analogy: two doctors. Doctor A: "Take this medicine, trust me." Doctor B: "Your blood test shows low iron, which causes your tiredness, so take this medicine." You can check and trust only Doctor B. Story: a student loan is rejected in seconds and nobody can say why, so you cannot fix it and nobody can check fairness.

Explainable AI (XAI) = methods that let humans understand why a model decided something.

2. Key goals of XAI (slides)

GoalMeaning (slides)Example
01 TrustVerify that predictions rely on legitimate domain logic rather than spurious correlationsIce-cream sales and sunburn both rise in summer; the real cause is sunny weather. A husky-vs-wolf model that was mostly looking at snow, not the animal, was right for the wrong reason and would fail on a husky in snow.
02 DebuggingIdentify where, how and why a model makes systematic errors on edge casesA medical model that fails on older patients: the explanation shows which feature misleads it
03 Fairness and auditingEnsure decisions do not discriminate on protected attributesA hiring model that weights gender, or a stand-in such as a name (a reported experimental recruiting tool marked down CVs with signs of being female and was stopped)

A spurious correlation is a pattern that exists in the data by accident and is not the real cause.

3. The accuracy vs interpretability trade-off (slides)

Model typeExamplesInterpretabilityPredictive power
Glass-box (intrinsic)Linear regression, decision trees, logistic regressionVery highModerate
Ensemble modelsRandom forest, XGBoost, LightGBMModerateHigh
Deep learningConvolutional nets, transformersLowVery high
  • Glass-box: the model itself is the explanation (intrinsic interpretability). Ensemble: a team of many small models combined; a random forest is many decision trees that vote; XGBoost and LightGBM build trees one after another, each fixing the earlier mistakes. One tree is readable; a hundred voting trees are not. Deep learning: many-layer networks (Lessons 6 and 7): powerful but unreadable (transformers can have billions of weights).
  • Solution: if we need a powerful black box, explain it afterwards with post-hoc methods (permutation importance, LIME, SHAP).

Intrinsic interpretability (slides)

  • Linear and logistic regression: direct mathematical transparency. Each feature coefficient directly represents the expected change in the outcome per unit increase in the feature value. Example: price = 10 + 5 × bedrooms + 0.2 × area. 3 bedrooms, 100 m²: 10 + 15 + 20 = 45; one more bedroom: +5; 10 m² more: +2. 2 bedrooms, 50 m²: 10 + 10 + 10 = 30. For logistic regression a positive coefficient raises the class probability.
  • Decision trees: explicit human-logic rules. IF-THEN conditional splits map directly to human logic. Loan tree: IF income ≥ 50,000 then (IF debt < 20,000 APPROVE ELSE REJECT) ELSE REJECT. Income 60,000 and debt 25,000 is rejected because "income is at least 50,000 but debt is not below 20,000"; income 70,000 and debt 10,000 is approved. Limits: very large trees stop being readable; glass-box models may be less accurate on complex problems such as images.

5. Permutation feature importance (slides, peer notes)

Core concept (slides)Measures how much model performance decreases when a feature's values are randomly shuffled. High impact: if shuffling breaks accuracy, the model relied heavily on that feature. Low impact: if performance stays unchanged, the feature was largely ignored.

Steps: measure the score on test data; shuffle one feature's column among the rows (breaks its link with the answer); measure again; importance = original score − new score; repeat for every feature and rank. Analogy: removing a team member.

Feature shuffledAccuracy after shufflingDrop = importanceMeaning
Hours studied62 %90 − 62 = 28High impact
Attendance80 %90 − 80 = 10Some impact
Shoe size90 %90 − 90 = 0Ignored by the model

Baseline accuracy 90 %. Good to know: works with any model (only needs inputs and predictions); global only; correlated features (height in cm and in inches) can hide each other; it shows that a feature matters, not how (higher or lower).

6. LIME (slides, peer notes)

LIME: Local Interpretable Model-agnostic ExplanationsLIME builds a simple, interpretable model (like linear regression) that mirrors the complex model's behaviour only in the immediate neighbourhood of a single prediction point. Local = one prediction; interpretable = the explaining model is readable; model-agnostic = works with any model.

Geometric analogy (slides): a complex topological map with winding curves; zoom in very close to one point and the surface looks flat; LIME draws a straight line across that local flat surface.

Steps: pick the prediction; create many slightly changed copies of the input; ask the model for their predictions; weight nearer copies more; fit a simple model (a line); read its coefficients. Worked example: model f(x) = x² at x = 3 (f = 9); nearby 2.9 → 8.41 and 3.1 → 9.61: slope = (9.61 − 8.41)/(3.1 − 2.9) = 1.20/0.2 = 6: near x = 3, each +1 in x raises the output by about 6. At x = 0 the true slope is 0, so the explanation is valid only locally. A loan example (illustrative): debt high −0.30, income medium +0.10, age 24 +0.02: high debt caused the rejection. Limits: one point only; random neighbours make results vary a little; the line is an approximation.

7. SHAP (slides, peer notes)

SHAP: game-theoretic interpretabilitySHAP adapts Shapley values from cooperative game theory to measure feature contribution. Analogy (slides): prediction = the winning team's cash prize; features = individual team players; SHAP calculates the exact reward each player deserves by testing all possible sub-coalitions of players with and without that feature.

Baseline: the model's average prediction knowing nothing about this person (here 50). Worked example (peer notes), a loan score: none known = 50; Income only = 70; Debt only = 60; Income and Debt = 90.

  • Income: income first adds 70 − 50 = 20; income after debt adds 90 − 60 = 30; average = (20 + 30)/2 = 25.
  • Debt: debt first adds 60 − 50 = 10; debt after income adds 90 − 70 = 20; average = 15.
  • Check: 25 + 15 = 40 = 90 − 50 (additivity). With three features all 6 orders are checked (e.g. +25, +15, +2 = 42 = 92 − 50).

Key axiomatic properties (slides): 1. Consistency: if a model changes so that a feature's contribution increases or stays the same, its SHAP value will not decrease (if income alone becomes 80 and income with debt 100, income's contributions are 30 and 40, SHAP 35 > 25, debt stays 15). 2. Additivity: the sum of the feature contributions equals the difference between the actual prediction and the average baseline prediction. Check: baseline 40, prediction 75, A = +20, so B = 35 − 20 = 15.

Cost: n features have 2n coalitions (8 for 3, 16 for 4, 1,024 for 10), so big models use shortcuts. Use: local (one person's values explain that decision) and global (average the size of SHAP values over many people); negative contributions push the score down.

  • An explanation is not a cause ("income mattered to the model" is not "income causes repayment"); explanations are approximations and can mislead; explaining a bad model does not make it good; show the right level of detail to the right audience (doctor, regulator, patient). Analogy: an X-ray shows what is inside but does not cure.

9. Ethical foundations of (Gen)AI (slides)

PrincipleMeaning (slides)Student example (peer notes)

XAI and ethics connect: accountability and transparency need you to be able to explain how a result came about; explanations (like SHAP) can reveal reliance on a protected attribute (fairness); a human can only oversee a decision they can understand.

10. The EU AI Act (assignment brief, peer notes)

The Artificial Intelligence Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the assignment links the EUR-Lex text), the EU's first big AI law; it entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. Analogy: traffic rules: a bicycle needs few rules, a car a licence and tests, a plane very strict checks: the riskier the AI, the stricter the rules.

LevelMeaningExamples
Unacceptable riskBanned outrightSocial scoring; manipulative AI exploiting weaknesses; untargeted scraping of face images; emotion recognition in workplaces and schools (narrow exceptions); real-time remote biometric identification in public for police (narrow exceptions)
High riskAllowed with strict dutiesHiring, education and exams, credit scoring, essential services, law enforcement, migration and border control, justice, critical infrastructure, some safety parts of products
Specific transparency risk (limited risk)Transparency dutiesChatbots (must say you are talking to AI); deepfakes and AI-generated images, audio, video (must be labelled)
Minimal riskNo specific dutiesSpam filters, AI in video games
  • High-risk duties: risk management across the life of the system; data governance (relevant, bias-checked training data); technical documentation; record-keeping (automatic logs); transparency (instructions for users); human oversight; accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity. These overlap with XAI: documentation, logs, transparency and oversight all depend on understanding what a system does.
  • General-purpose AI (GPAI) models (e.g. large language models): technical documentation, information for downstream builders, a copyright policy and a summary of training content; models with systemic risk (presumed above 1025 training operations) face extra duties (evaluations, adversarial testing, incident reporting, cybersecurity). A voluntary Code of Practice was published in July 2025.
  • Enforcement and fines: European AI Office and national authorities. Prohibited practices: up to €35 million or 7 % of worldwide yearly turnover (the higher); most other duties: €15 million or 3 %; false information to authorities: €7.5 million or 1 %.
  • Timeline (changing): 1 Aug 2024 in force; 2 Feb 2025 bans and AI-literacy duty; 2 Aug 2025 GPAI rules and governance; 2 Aug 2026 general application incl. transparency duties; the strictest high-risk rules were planned for 2026 but recent summaries say an EU amendment (the "Digital Omnibus") postponed them (2 Dec 2027 for the listed use areas, 2 Aug 2028 for AI in regulated products). Check the official text before quoting dates.

Written questions

W1. Explain the black-box problem and the three goals of explainable AI with an example for each.

Modern ML prioritises predictive performance over transparency; complex models hide decision logic in millions of parameters, which is risky in medicine (life safety), credit scoring (financial loss) and driving (legal liability). XAI goals: Trust (verify legitimate logic, e.g. a husky vs wolf model that actually looked at snow); Debugging (find where, how and why systematic errors occur on edge cases, e.g. failures on older patients); Fairness and auditing (check decisions do not discriminate on protected attributes, e.g. a hiring tool using gender or a stand-in).

W2. Describe the accuracy vs interpretability trade-off and how glass-box models and post-hoc methods fit in.

Glass-box models (linear / logistic regression, decision trees) have very high interpretability and moderate power; ensembles (random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM) moderate and high; deep learning (CNNs, transformers) low and very high. Glass-box models explain themselves (coefficients, IF-THEN rules). When we need a black box, post-hoc methods such as permutation importance, LIME and SHAP explain it afterwards.

W3. Distinguish global and local interpretability and say which methods suit each.

Global explains the overall logic across the dataset (which features drive predictions overall): permutation importance, averaged SHAP. Local explains one prediction (why was this loan rejected?): LIME and SHAP. Global suits engineers and auditors; local suits the affected person.

W4. A model has baseline accuracy 90 %. Shuffling hours studied gives 62 %, attendance 80 %, shoe size 90 %. Compute and interpret the permutation importances.

Importance = baseline − shuffled accuracy: hours 28, attendance 10, shoe size 0. The model relied heavily on hours studied, somewhat on attendance and ignored shoe size. Caveats: it is global, works with any model, and correlated features can hide each other.

W5. Explain LIME and compute the local slope for f(x) = x² at x = 3 using points 2.9 and 3.1.

LIME fits a simple interpretable model (such as a line) to the complex model's predictions for many perturbed copies near one point, weighting nearer copies more; the coefficients are the explanation. f(2.9) = 8.41, f(3.1) = 9.61; slope = (9.61 − 8.41)/0.2 = 6, so near x = 3 each +1 in x raises the output by about 6. It is valid only locally (at x = 0 the true slope is 0).

W6. Explain SHAP and compute the values for Income and Debt given baseline 50, Income only 70, Debt only 60, both 90. State its two axioms.

SHAP uses Shapley values: each feature's average marginal contribution over all orders (coalitions). Income: first adds 20, after Debt adds 30, average 25. Debt: first adds 10, after Income adds 20, average 15. 25 + 15 = 40 = 90 − 50. Axioms: consistency (if a feature's contribution increases or stays the same its SHAP value does not decrease) and additivity (contributions sum to prediction minus baseline). Cost grows as 2n.

W7. List the nine ethical foundations of (Gen)AI and explain how XAI supports three of them.

Integrity, originality, transparency, accountability, compliance, security, intellectual property, fairness and bias, human oversight. XAI supports transparency and accountability (you can only be accountable for what you can explain), fairness (SHAP can reveal reliance on a protected attribute) and human oversight (humans can only supervise decisions they understand).

W8. Summarise the EU AI Act's risk levels with examples, the duties of high-risk systems and the maximum fines.

Unacceptable risk (banned): social scoring, manipulative AI, untargeted face scraping. High risk: hiring, education, credit scoring, law enforcement, critical infrastructure; duties: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy / robustness / cybersecurity. Specific transparency (limited) risk: chatbots and deepfakes must be disclosed or labelled. Minimal risk: spam filters, games. GPAI models have documentation, copyright and systemic-risk duties. Fines up to €35 million or 7 % of turnover for prohibited practices, €15 million or 3 % for most other duties, €7.5 million or 1 % for false information.
