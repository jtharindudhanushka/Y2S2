Prompt Engineering

Covers: what a prompt is, why prompt engineering matters, the TRADE framework, the five prompting techniques (zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, role, constraint), best practices and limits, and how prompt engineering fits with context, harness and loop engineering. No prior lecture attendance needed.

Learning outcomes

  • Define a prompt and prompt engineering, and explain why it matters
  • Build a prompt with the TRADE framework
  • Use the five techniques: zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, role and constraint prompting
  • State best practices, applications and limitations
  • Relate prompt engineering to context, harness and loop engineering

1. What is a prompt?

DefinitionA prompt is the text instruction or question you give to an AI model to get a response. Prompt engineering is the process of creating clear and effective inputs for an AI system, structuring your instructions so the AI understands your context and delivers what you need, ideally on the first try.

The AI cannot read your mind; it only knows what you tell it. Analogy: a taxi driver. "Take me somewhere" gets nowhere useful; "take me to the airport by the fastest road, I have 2 bags" gets what you need. The AI is the driver, your prompt is the directions.

Vague prompt vs clear prompt

VagueClear
"Write an email about saving money."

The AI does not know who is writing, who will read it, how long it should be or what style to use, so it guesses and produces a long, dry, generic email.		"You are a friendly finance coach. Write a short email with 3 tips to save money on groceries, for busy college students. Under 150 words, casual tone, bullet points."

It answers the questions the AI would otherwise guess: who am I, what must I do, who is reading, how long, what style.

Another pair: weak prompt "Explain photosynthesis." Improved: "Explain photosynthesis in simple terms for a grade 8 student in about 100 words." (it now has an audience and a length constraint).

Why is it important?

  • Improves accuracy by reducing vague or irrelevant responses.
  • Saves time: useful results in fewer attempts.
  • Enhances creativity when generating ideas, stories or designs.
  • Supports problem solving by guiding the AI through logical steps.
  • Increases reliability in tasks such as summarisation, coding and analysis.

2. The TRADE framework

To move away from generic answers, use the TRADE blueprint. Analogy: briefing a new employee: tell them the task, who they act as, who the customer is, the rules, and show an example of good work.

LetterPartWhat to includeSample
TTask (the what)Say exactly what to do with a direct, specific action verb: write, summarise, draft, analyse, reformat, brainstorm, explain."Write a newsletter email with 3 quick tips on saving on weekly groceries."
RRole (the who)Give the AI a persona or expert identity; it changes how it reasons and which vocabulary it uses (senior software engineer vs 5th-grade science teacher)."You are a friendly personal finance coach for young adults."
AAudience (the whom)Who will read or use the output; sets the complexity (complete beginner vs corporate executives)."The audience is busy college students on a tight budget."
DDetails (constraints)Rules, context and format: structure (table, bullet points), length (under 200 words), tone (witty, professional, urgent)."Under 150 words, casual encouraging tone, clear bullet points."
EExample (the model)A sample of what a successful output looks like; this is few-shot prompting: the pattern is mirrored."Format like: Tip 1: [Catchy Name] – [1-sentence action step]"

How TRADE matches the lecturer's "elements of a good prompt": clear instruction = Task; context (background) = Role and Audience; specific details (format, length, audience, style) and constraints (word count, tone, scope) = Details; examples = Example.

Build a prompt in 5 steps

StepPrompt so far
TaskExplain photosynthesis.
+ RoleYou are a friendly science teacher. Explain photosynthesis.
+ Audience... for Grade 6 students.
+ Details... in under 120 words, using simple words and 4 bullet points.
+ Example... Use this style: "Step 1: Sunlight. Leaves catch it."

Each step makes the answer more exact.

Check yourself: in "You are a vet. Write 5 bullet tips on feeding puppies for first-time owners, under 100 words": Role = a vet; Task = write 5 tips on feeding puppies; Audience = first-time owners; Details = 5 bullets, under 100 words.

3. Prompting techniques

#TechniqueMeaningExample
1Zero-shotAsk the AI to perform a task without examples."Say if this review is Positive or Negative: 'Love the camera'."
2Few-shotProvide a few examples before asking the AI to complete a similar task, so it copies the pattern.Review "Great phone" → Positive; "Battery dies fast" → Negative; "Love the camera" → ? (answer Positive)
3Chain-of-thoughtEncourage the AI to explain its reasoning step by step."...Think step by step."
4Role promptingAssign a role."Act as a teacher" / "act as a software engineer"
5Constraint-basedAdd rules to limit the answer."Explain in 100 words."

Chain-of-thought example

Plain: "A pen costs 20 rupees. I buy 3 pens and pay with a 100 rupee note. What is my change?" Chain-of-thought adds "Think step by step", and the AI reasons: 3 pens cost 3 × 20 = 60 rupees; change = 100 − 60 = 40 rupees. Showing the steps makes mistakes easier to spot (like a teacher saying "show your working").

5. Beyond prompts: the four engineering skills

The lecturer's AI Kitchen guide uses a celebrity chef (who knows every recipe but has never been inside your restaurant) to show that a good prompt is only the first of four pillars of AI engineering.

PillarKitchen storyAI world
Prompt engineeringBad order: "Make me some food" (the chef might cook luxury sushi). Engineered order: "Please make a breakfast egg-and-cheese sandwich on a toasted brioche bun. Do not add bacon."The clearer, more specific and well-structured the instruction typed into the model (ChatGPT, Gemini), the better the response.
Context engineeringThe chef does not know your internal rules and would guess the signature burger from other restaurants. You open the company manual to page 5: "exactly two slices of cheddar and our secret house sauce."Automatically finding the exact data, documents or database records the AI needs and embedding them in the prompt window so it does not guess or hallucinate. Often implemented as RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation).
Harness engineeringThe chef is just a brain and cannot act without tools, but unmonitored access is dangerous. Build a controlled prep station: three pre-approved tools (spatula, pan, timer), lock the chemical closet, install an automatic fire extinguisher that cuts the gas.Secure back-end infrastructure code (Python, TypeScript, isolated Docker containers) that gives the AI digital "hands" (APIs, file systems) while trapping it in a sandbox so it cannot run dangerous commands.
Loop engineeringDo not serve the first burger. Act (cook), Observe (scale and camera: cheese not melted), Evaluate (a Checker AI: fails the standard), Loop (send back: 30 more seconds on the grill), Terminate (leaves only when it passes all tests).Designing agentic state workflows (e.g. with LangGraph) where an agent executes a task, checks its work against automated tests, rewrites its approach and self-corrects until a strict success criterion is met.

Quick review (from the guide): Prompt = C (the clear, specific order); Context = A (the manual to read); Harness = D (the safe workbench and tools); Loop = B (the review loop until perfect). Loop engineering leads naturally into the agent loop of Lesson 3.

Key takeaways A prompt is the instruction you give an AI; prompt engineering makes it clear and effective. TRADE = Task, Role, Audience, Details, Example. Techniques: zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, role, constraint. Prompts guide but never guarantee: verify facts. Prompt, context (RAG), harness (sandbox, tools) and loop (self-correction) are the four pillars of AI engineering.

Written questions

W1. Define prompt engineering and explain five reasons it is important.

Prompt engineering is the process of creating clear and effective inputs for an AI system to guide it toward the desired output. Important because it improves accuracy by reducing vague or irrelevant responses, saves time (useful results in fewer attempts), enhances creativity, supports problem solving by guiding the AI through logical steps, and increases reliability in summarisation, coding and analysis.

W2. Explain the TRADE framework and rewrite 'Write an email about saving money' using it.

TRADE = Task (what, with an action verb), Role (who the AI acts as), Audience (who reads it), Details (length, format, tone, constraints), Example (a sample output, i.e. few-shot). Rewrite: Role: You are a friendly finance coach. Task: Write a short email with 3 tips to save money on groceries. Audience: busy college students. Details: under 150 words, casual tone, bullet points. Example: format as 'Tip 1: [Catchy Name] - [one-sentence action]'.

W3. Describe the five prompting techniques with an example of each.

Zero-shot: a task with no examples ('Classify this review as positive or negative'). Few-shot: a few solved examples first. Chain-of-thought: ask for step-by-step reasoning ('Think step by step'). Role prompting: assign a role ('Act as a teacher'). Constraint-based: add rules ('Explain in 100 words').

W4. State the best practices and limitations of prompt engineering.

Best practices: use simple and direct language, provide enough context, specify the expected output format, avoid ambiguous or overly broad instructions. Limitations: responses may contain errors or outdated information; poorly designed prompts can lead to biased, incomplete or irrelevant outputs; it does not guarantee perfect results.

W5. Using the AI Kitchen analogy, explain prompt, context, harness and loop engineering.

Prompt: the clear order given to the chef. Context: the company recipe book placed beside the chef (in AI, RAG supplies the right documents so the model does not hallucinate). Harness: the safe prep station, approved tools and fire extinguisher (in AI, sandboxed infrastructure and tools such as APIs and file systems). Loop: act, observe, evaluate, loop and terminate quality control (in AI, an agent checks its own work against tests and self-corrects, e.g. with LangGraph).
