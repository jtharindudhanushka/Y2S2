1. What is AI?

DefinitionArtificial Intelligence (AI) is the science of developing techniques to generate intelligence artificially: building machines that can think like humans, that is reason, learn, perceive and decide.

A calculator is brilliant at one thing (arithmetic) but cannot recognise a face. A person can learn almost anything. AI sits between the two, which is why we describe it in three levels.

The three levels of AI

LevelAlso calledMeaningExamples
ANIWeak AIHighly specialised AI that excels at one specific task (or a limited set) under strict constraints; cannot apply its intelligence outside its domain.Face recognition, chess engines, language translation, Siri / Alexa, Google Maps, spam filters
AGIStrong AI, human-level AICan think, learn and perform any intellectual task a human can, and learn new tasks without retraining. Not yet achieved.Hypothetical: a robot that can study, cook, drive and solve problems like a human
ASISuperintelligenceFar more intelligent than humans in every field: science, creativity, decision making, emotional intelligence, social skills. A future concept.None yet
Memory trickNarrow = one job. General = any job, like a human. Super = better than every human. Only ANI exists today.

Older names, same idea. Strong AI: the machine is truly thinking like a human brain (functionality of a real brain). Weak AI: the machine pretends to be intelligent and performs a function (browsing, disease detection, Amazon recommendations). Humans are said to have a "carbon brain" and AI a "silicon brain".

Why is AI called a disruptive technology?

A disruptive technology changes the normal way things are done and replaces older methods; it does not merely improve existing tools but reshapes industries. Examples: AI, smartphones (they replaced cameras, maps, shopping habits), cloud computing, blockchain and the internet. Blockchain is itself a disruptive idea: centralisation (one authority controls the data) was the base of modern technology, and blockchain introduced decentralisation (no single owner) as an alternative. Both coexist, depending on the use case.

Why study AI? To face disruptive technologies and to create new products and tools; AI can give better solutions to the problems we face, often by optimisation (finding the best answer).

2. A short history

WhenWhat happened
1939–1945 (WWII)War turned abstract ideas about thinking machines into urgent, funded problems. Alan Turing helped design the Bombe at Bletchley Park to break the German Enigma cipher (a machine doing what seemed to need human reasoning). Colossus (1943–44, Lorenz cipher) and ENIAC (1945, artillery tables) were among the first electronic computers. WWII supplied computers, funding and urgency, and scientists who had seen machines do "intelligent" work.
1943McCulloch and Pitts publish the first mathematical model of an artificial neuron: networks of simple neuron-like units can compute logical functions (ancestor of neural networks, Lesson 6). The lecture notes also mention a successful neuron architecture around this era.
1948Norbert Wiener's book Cybernetics: systems that sense, adjust and act in a loop (like a thermostat). The same loop defines intelligent agents today.
1950Turing's paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence asks "Can machines think?" and proposes the Turing Test.
1956Dartmouth Conference (a summer research project, about 9 months of discussion on building a "mechanical brain"). John McCarthy coined the term Artificial Intelligence (and later created LISP); Marvin Minsky co-founded the field and the MIT AI Lab. The conference decided that symbol processing (if ... then ...) was the best way to build the brain.
1959Arthur Samuel (IBM) coined Machine Learning: computers learning without being explicitly programmed. His checkers program learned to beat its creator.

The Turing Test

A human interrogator (a person who asks questions to evaluate or gather information from another person or system) chats in writing with two hidden players: a human and a machine. The interrogator cannot see inside. If the interrogator cannot reliably tell which is the machine, the machine passes and is said to be intelligent. It tests whether a machine behaves like a human, not whether it truly understands.

The Chinese Room argument (John Searle)

A person who does not understand Chinese sits in a room with a very powerful rule book (a knowledge base). Questions in Chinese come in; the person follows the rules (a mapping function f(x) from question to answer) and passes out perfect Chinese answers. From outside it looks as if they understand Chinese, but inside nobody understands anything; the rules are just followed. Searle's point: a computer can act intelligent without being intelligent, so it could pass the Turing Test through rule-following alone. (Analogy: a parrot reciting perfect sentences without knowing what they mean.)

3. Two ways to build AI

Rule-based (GOFAI)Data-driven (Machine Learning)

IdeaExperts write facts and rules with symbols; the machine follows them.Nobody writes the rules. Show many examples; the machine finds the patterns. Data is fed continuously.

ToolsProlog (rule based), LISP (not rule based by default, but can implement rule-based AI; McCarthy created it for this), expert systemsNeural networks, GPUs / TPUs, cloud computing
AnalogyCooking from a recipe bookA child learning "dog" by seeing many dogs

An expert system stores an expert's if-then rules (e.g. "if fever and rash then possible measles"). The history of AI is largely a shift from GOFAI to the data-driven approach, which dominates today (the data-driven idea itself dates from the 1940s: find patterns in the environment from a continuous feed of data).

Why does data-driven AI work so well now?

  1. Availability of data (the big data explosion): phones, websites and sensors generate vast amounts of data.
  2. Massive computing power: deep learning needs heavy matrix maths. GPUs (thousands of small calculations at once), TPUs (Google's AI chips) and scalable cloud computing (renting powerful computers over the internet) made it feasible.

Big data and the 3 Vs (Doug Laney, 2001)

VMeaning
VolumeHow much data: the scale generated and stored every second.
VelocityHow fast new data is created, streamed and must be processed.
VarietyHow many types: tables, text, images, video, audio.

Analogy: a river. Volume = how much water; velocity = how fast it flows; variety = what floats in it.

How a machine learns: train, then test

Build a model (the pattern learned) from training data, then judge it on testing data it has never seen. Two examples from the "gentle introduction to ML" idea: a decision tree (a set of yes/no questions ending in a prediction; classification) and a line fitted to dots (e.g. yam eaten vs 100 m running speed; regression).

Which method is better? Compare the total error on training and on testing data:

ModelError on training dataError on testing data
Straight line812
Wiggly squiggle through every dot030

(Illustrative numbers.) The squiggle looks perfect on training data but is far worse on new data: this is overfitting (it memorised the training dots including their random wiggles). The straight line is the better model. Analogy: memorising practice papers and failing the real exam. Choose a method by how well it predicts the testing data, not by how fancy it is. 4. The AI map

  • Neural network (NN): layers of simple connected units passing numbers along, loosely inspired by the brain.
  • Deep learning (DL): a neural network with many layers ("deep" = many layers).
  • Transformer: a deep learning model that reads all the words of a text together and decides which words matter to each other (used in ChatGPT).
  • Beside ML sit other AI methods: Genetic Algorithms (copy evolution; an extended form of search), Swarm Intelligence (copy ants and birds), Fuzzy Logic (binary linguistic rules; degrees of true), rule-based systems (GOFAI). Methods can be hybridised, e.g. Learning Classifier Systems (LCS) combine ideas from genetic algorithms and ML.

Course outline (as the lecturer listed it): history of AI, agents, search (when AI is applied to a problem there is always a search; there is optimisation in any AI application), genetic algorithms, machine learning, neural networks (the dominant approach today), rule-based systems, fuzzy logic, swarm intelligence, basics of reinforcement learning.

Analogy: predictive AI is an examiner who labels things; generative AI is an artist who makes something new.

6. How an AI system interacts with the world

  • Sensors collect information from the environment (camera, microphone, bump sensor); actuators perform actions in it (motors, wheels, outputs).
  • Embodied AI has a physical body: self-driving cars, humanoid robots, a robot vacuum. Disembodied AI is software only: ChatGPT, Google Translate, a spam filter.

You do the same: eyes and ears sense, your brain decides, hands and legs act, then you look again. Systems built around this loop are agents, covered in Lesson 3.

Key takeaways AI = developing techniques to generate intelligence artificially. Only ANI exists; AGI and ASI are hypothetical. AI was named at Dartmouth in 1956 (McCarthy); ML named by Samuel in 1959. Turing Test = behaviour; Chinese Room = behaviour is not understanding. GOFAI = hand-written rules; data-driven = learns from data, powered by big data (3 Vs) and GPUs / TPUs / cloud. AI ⊃ ML ⊃ NN ⊃ DL. Predictive (classification, regression, forecasting) vs generative. Systems sense → decide → act. Choose models by testing-data performance (avoid overfitting).
