Fuzzy Logic Systems

Covers: why fuzzy logic (vague human assessments, Zadeh 1965, the Sendai subway), crisp vs fuzzy sets, terminology (membership function, linguistic variable, hedge), fuzzy propositional logic, union / intersection / complement, fuzzy rules and system components, fuzzification and membership functions (triangular, trapezoidal, L/R, Gaussian), Mamdani and Sugeno inference, defuzzification by centre of gravity, and two worked controllers. No prior lecture attendance needed.

Learning outcomes

  • Explain why fuzzy logic is needed and contrast crisp (Boolean) logic and sets with fuzzy logic and sets
  • Use the terminology: fuzzy set, membership function, fuzzy / linguistic variable, linguistic hedge, fuzzy proposition
  • Apply fuzzy union (max), intersection (min) and complement (1 − μ)
  • Write fuzzy IF-THEN rules and describe the four system components (fuzzifier, inference, defuzzifier, rule base)
  • Compute triangular, trapezoidal, L/R and Gaussian membership values
  • Run Mamdani (clip, aggregate) and Sugeno inference, then defuzzify with the centre of gravity

Sources: the lecturer's slides (Fuzzy Logic Systems, 40 slides, marked (slides)) and the peer-made lesson (worked examples, marked (peer notes)).

1. What is fuzzy logic and why? (slides)

DefinitionFuzzy logic is a technique that allows the inclusion of vague human assessments in computing problems. It can be used to build intelligent systems for decision making, identification, pattern recognition, optimisation and control (Singh et al., 2013).
  • Binary (Boolean) vs fuzzy: "Is it cold?" Boolean logic answers Yes/1 or No/0. Fuzzy logic answers with degrees, e.g. Very much 0.9, Little 0.25, Very less 0.1. Analogy: a light switch vs a dimmer.
  • Restaurant tip: do you measure the taste of the food and the quality of service and compute the tip, or use linguistic rules such as: 1. If service is poor or food is bad, then tip is cheap. 2. If service is good, then tip is average. 3. If service is excellent or food is delicious, then tip is generous. The second is closer to the human way of thinking, and the resulting service/food → tip surface is non-linear.
  • History: developed by Lotfi Zadeh in 1965; based on a system of non-digital (continuous, fuzzy, without crisp boundaries) set theory and rules.
  • Advantages: an accurate quantitative model is not required to control a plant or decide an action; it deals with vague systems using linguistic variables; leads to faster and simpler development of controllers; can be a decision-support tool for managers.
  • Fuzzy logic boom in Japan: Sendai Subway automatic train operation control, 1987: a fuzzy system controls acceleration, deceleration and braking, reduces energy consumption by 10 % and passengers hardly feel the change of velocity. It considers: acceleration to a target speed with velocity oscillation, maintaining target speed (coasting), and stopping at the correct location with velocity control for a no-motion feel.
  • In machines: an air conditioner periodically checks the room temperature against the target temperature and decides whether to cool, heat or leave unchanged.

2. Crisp sets vs fuzzy sets and the terminology (slides, peer notes)

Crisp setFuzzy set
An element is either a member or not (membership 0 or 1). Most things in life are crisp: "it's a car", "the car is Red".An element has a partial membership. The distance to the car in front can be a partial member of both "far" and "close". "Sue is tall and short!"
"Temperature at least 30 °C": 29.9 is out (0), 30 is in (1): a tiny change flips the decisionA temperature gradually becomes warmer, then hotter: degrees between 0 and 1
TermMeaning
Fuzzy setLet X be a set. X is a fuzzy set if each element y is characterised by a membership function μX(y): X → [0, 1], the degree of membership of y in X. Example: a given colour has 0.38 membership in the set Green.
Fuzzy variableA variable with fuzzy sets as its values
Linguistic variableA fuzzy variable whose values are words or sentences (temperature: freezing, cool, warm, hot)
Linguistic hedgeA modifier of basic terms (very, somewhat, extremely). Peer rule: μvery A(x) = μA(x)², so very warm at μ = 0.6 is 0.36 (always state the rule used)
Universe of discourseThe range of values being described (e.g. temperatures 0 to 50 °C)

Classical vs fuzzy example (slides): Set A = faculty members of a department; Set B = faculty members who are young (below 40). In a crisp set B contains only {x1, x2, x3} (ages 32, 28, 38). In a fuzzy implementation x4 (age 42) and x5 (age 49) get partial memberships: μB(x) = 1/x1 + 1/x2 + 1/x3 + 0.7/x4 + 0.3/x5 (read "degree / element"; the + is just a list).

Do not confuseA fuzzy degree is not a probability. μwarm(28) = 0.7 does not mean "70 % chance it is warm"; the temperature is known exactly and the chosen "warm" curve gives 0.7 there. Probability is about uncertainty; fuzzy degree is about how much something fits a soft category.

3. Propositional logic and fuzzy propositional logic (slides)

  • Propositional logic uses truth statements to form other truth statements (it was used in the forward-chaining algorithm). Simple sentences are propositional constants with truth values (Cat is a mammal; All lecturers are males). Compound sentences are built with negation, conjunction, disjunction, implication, reduction and equivalence (Sky is cloudy AND wind is strong).
  • The slides recall the logical equivalences: ∧ and ∨ are commutative and associative; double negation ¬(¬A) ≡ A; contraposition (A ⇒ B) ≡ (¬B ⇒ ¬A); implication elimination (A ⇒ B) ≡ (¬A ∨ B); biconditional elimination; De Morgan: ¬(A ∧ B) ≡ (¬A ∨ ¬B) and ¬(A ∨ B) ≡ (¬A ∧ ¬B); distributivity of ∧ over ∨ and of ∨ over ∧.
  • Fuzzy propositional logic: the membership degree μX(y) is the truth value of the sentence y in the universe of discourse X: 0 = absolute falsity, 1 = absolute truth. A fuzzy proposition such as "Joe is short" has the fuzzy truth value μshort(Joe): Joe is a member of the set of short people with that degree.
  • Caution (peer notes): not every classical law carries over unchanged. "A OR NOT A" is always true in normal logic, but with A = 0.6: max(0.6, 0.4) = 0.6, not 1.

4. Fuzzy set operations (slides)

Example: warm = 0.6, humid = 0.8: warm AND humid = 0.6; warm OR humid = 0.8; NOT warm = 0.4. A = 0.3, B = 0.9: A AND B = 0.3, A OR B = 0.9, NOT A = 0.7. (Other operator families exist, e.g. product for AND; state which you use.)

5. Fuzzy rules and the system components (slides)

If antecedent(s) Then consequent(s)The antecedents of a rule combine fuzzy sets with the logic operators (complement, intersection, union), so the crisp inputs must be fuzzified first. The consequent is usually a single fuzzy set with its membership function.
  • Crisp input → Fuzzifier (membership functions give degrees) → Inference (uses the fuzzy sets and the fuzzy rules) → Defuzzifier (back to a number) → Crisp output.
  • Air-conditioner rules (slides): 1. IF (temperature is cold OR too-cold) AND (target is warm) THEN command is heat. 2. IF (temperature is hot OR too-hot) AND (target is warm) THEN command is cool. 3. IF (temperature is warm) AND (target is warm) THEN command is no-change. The rules are prepared by human experts (an expert-system style knowledge base).

Crisp expert system vs fuzzy system (peer notes): an expert system stores expert IF-THEN rules in a knowledge base used by an inference engine; the crisp version fires or does not, the fuzzy version fires partly and several rules at once. They are not opposites: a fuzzy rule base written by experts is both. Neither learns by itself unless learning is added.

6. Fuzzification and membership functions (slides, peer notes)

Fuzzification converts crisp input values into fuzzy sets (linguistic variables) through membership functions. Example: temperature is described as Freezing, Cool, Warm or Hot; belonging to a set cannot be defined by a threshold point; fuzzification defines how much a value belongs to each linguistic variable (the slide asks "how cool is 36 °F?": it is partly Cool and partly Freezing).

Types of membership functions: triangular, trapezoidal, singleton, Gaussian, piecewise linear, and L / R functions.

FunctionParameters and formula (slides)
TriangularLower limit a, peak m, upper limit b (a < m < b). μ(x) = 0 for x ≤ a; (x − a)/(m − a) for a < x ≤ m; (b − x)/(b − m) for m < x < b; 0 for x ≥ b
Trapezoidala < b < c < d. 0 outside (a, d); (x − a)/(b − a) for a ≤ x ≤ b; 1 for b ≤ x ≤ c; (d − x)/(d − c) for c ≤ x ≤ d
R-function (a = b = −∞)1 for x < c; (d − x)/(d − c) for c ≤ x ≤ d; 0 for x > d (high on the left, falling)
L-function (c = d = +∞)0 for x < a; (x − a)/(b − a) for a ≤ x ≤ b; 1 for x > b (rising, then stays high: a shoulder)
GaussianCentral value m, standard deviation k > 0 (smaller k = narrower bell): μ(x) = exp( −(x − m)² / (2k²) )
SingletonNon-zero at one point only (useful for a single output value)

Worked examples (peer notes): triangle (20, 25, 30): at 23 °C: (23 − 20)/(25 − 20) = 0.6; at 25: 1; at 27: (30 − 27)/5 = 0.6; at 28: 0.4. Triangle (10, 20, 30) at 15: 0.5. Trapezoid (20, 25, 30, 35): at 22: 0.4; at 28: 1; at 33: 0.4. Shoulder "hot" rising from 0 at 25 °C to 1 at 30 °C: at 28: 0.6. Gaussian m = 25, σ = 3, x = 28: exp(−9/18) = exp(−0.5) = 0.607. Fuzzifying 28 °C with warm = triangle (20, 25, 30) and hot = shoulder (25 to 30): warm = 0.4, hot = 0.6, so both the "warm" and the "hot" rule fire.

7. Inference (slides)

Inference = a set of rules is evaluated to derive the output; evaluation needs the fuzzy set operations. The most commonly used methods are Mamdani's and Sugeno's (Takagi-Sugeno); they mainly differ in the way they take the output. (The slide's tree: fuzzy reasoning → direct methods: Mamdani's direct method, Takagi and Sugeno's method, simplified method; and indirect methods.)

  1. Evaluate the antecedent (applied for each rule). Fuzzify the inputs, then apply the operator: AND = min, OR = max. Slide example (restaurant, service = 3, food = 8): "service is excellent" = 0.0; "food is delicious" = 0.7; antecedent "service is excellent OR food is delicious" = max(0.0, 0.7) = 0.7 (the rule's firing strength). A rule with strength 0 does not contribute.
  2. Obtain the conclusion (implication). Use the minimum, which truncates (clips) the consequent's membership function at the rule strength: min(α, μconsequent(y)). The product can be used instead and multiplies (scales) the membership function. Analogy: a ceiling over a hill. The slide's rule: if service is excellent or food is delicious then tip is generous: the generous curve is clipped at 0.7.
  3. Combine multiple rules (aggregation). Commonly used aggregation operators: the maximum, the sum and the probabilistic sum. In the slide's example with three rules (service poor or food rancid → tip cheap; service good → tip average; service excellent or food delicious → tip generous) the three clipped curves are combined using max into one result of aggregation.
  4. Defuzzify the aggregated curve (section 8).

Worked point (peer notes): "medium" fires at 0.4 and "high" at 0.6; at some y the original curves are 0.9 and 0.5: clipped medium = min(0.4, 0.9) = 0.4; clipped high = min(0.6, 0.5) = 0.5; combined (max) = 0.5. A rule firing at 0.3 on a curve of 0.8 gives 0.3.

8. Defuzzification (slides)

The result of inference (the control output) is still vague. Defuzzification converts the fuzzy output back to the crisp output suitable for the real application. Notable methods: first of maximum, middle of maximum, last of maximum, mean of maximum, centre of gravity (centroid). The maxima methods pick from the highest points of the combined curve and can differ when there are two peaks.

Centre of gravity (centroid), the most popular techniqueThe weighted average of the membership function (or the area bounded by the curve) is computed: COG = Σ μ(x) · x / Σ μ(x); if x is continuous, COG = ∫ μ(x) x dx / ∫ μ(x) dx. It is the balance point of the cut-out shape.

Slide example: the restaurant aggregated output defuzzifies to tip = 16.7 %. Peer example: degrees 0.2, 0.5, 0.3 at speeds 20, 50, 80: (4 + 25 + 24)/1.0 = 53. Degrees 0.5, 0.5 at speeds 40, 60: 50.

Sugeno (zero-order) shortcut (peer notes): the THEN part is a constant z; the output is the weighted average Σ αi zi / Σ αi. Rule 1 z = 50 at 0.4 and rule 2 z = 80 at 0.6: (20 + 48)/1.0 = 68.

MamdaniSugeno
THEN partA fuzzy set (a curve)A constant or a function of the inputs
StepsClip, aggregate, then defuzzify (centroid)Weighted average of the constants
StrengthEasy to explain in wordsConvenient for optimisation and numeric control

If no rule fires, every strength is 0 and the formulas divide by 0: a safe design needs rule coverage or a fallback rule.

9. Full worked example A: fan controller at 28 °C (peer notes)

Inputs: Warm = triangle (20, 25, 30) °C; Hot = 0 at 25 °C rising to 1 at 30 °C and 1 above. Output (fan speed 0 to 100): Medium = triangle (30, 50, 70); High = 0 at 50 rising to 1 at 80, then 1. Rule 1: IF warm THEN fan is medium. Rule 2: IF hot THEN fan is high.

  1. Fuzzify 28 °C: warm = (30 − 28)/5 = 0.4; hot = (28 − 25)/5 = 0.6.
  2. Fire: rule 1 at 0.4, rule 2 at 0.6.
  3. Clip and combine (max) on speeds every 10 units:

10. Full worked example B: the heater controller (slides)

An air conditioner / heater has two inputs: the current temperature T (LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH; trapezoids over about 20–90 °F) and the temperature change rate ΔT (LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH; °F per hour, 0 to 5); the output is the heater motor speed (SLOW, MEDIUM, FAST; R/M, 100 to 1000). The fuzzy control rules as a table (T across, ΔT down):

11. Applications and the open question (slides)

Uses seen: train control (Sendai subway), air conditioners and heaters, restaurant tipping, decision support for managers, pattern recognition. The final slide asks: "What are the data-analytics applications of fuzzy logic? Can fuzzy logic be used for image annotation?" (its reference is about intelligent tagging of online texts using fuzzy logic). Suggested answer: yes: a picture can belong to several tags to different degrees (e.g. 0.8 "beach", 0.5 "sunset", 0.1 "crowd"), so soft, degree-based labels, fuzzy rules combining features (colour, texture) and defuzzified ranking of tags suit annotation, tagging and recommendation where categories overlap.

Limits (peer notes): a fuzzy system is understandable but does not learn rules or curves by itself; rules need experts and testing (gaps, contradictions, units, sensor errors, no-rule-fires cases).

Written questions

W1. Explain the difference between crisp and fuzzy sets and logic, with examples, and state two advantages of fuzzy control.

A crisp set: membership is 0 or 1 ('the car is red'; hot = temperature at least 30 °C, so 29.9 and 30 are totally different). A fuzzy set: partial membership in [0, 1] (distance to the car ahead is partly 'close' and partly 'far'; μB = 1/x1 + … + 0.7/x4 + 0.3/x5 for 'young'). Boolean logic answers yes/no; fuzzy logic gives degrees (0.9, 0.25, 0.1). Advantages: no accurate quantitative model is required, deals with vague systems using linguistic variables, closer to human thinking, faster and simpler controller development (Sendai subway: 10 % less energy, smooth ride).

W2. Define fuzzy set, fuzzy variable, linguistic variable, linguistic hedge and fuzzy proposition.

Fuzzy set: each element y has a membership μX(y) in [0, 1]. Fuzzy variable: a variable whose values are fuzzy sets. Linguistic variable: a fuzzy variable with word values (freezing, cool, warm, hot). Linguistic hedge: a modifier such as very or somewhat (very A = μ²). Fuzzy proposition: a sentence like 'Joe is short' whose truth value is the membership μshort(Joe), 0 meaning absolute falsity and 1 absolute truth.

W3. Give the fuzzy union, intersection and complement formulas and compute them for μA = 0.3 and μB = 0.9.

Union max(μA, μB) = 0.9; intersection min = 0.3; complement of A = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7.

W4. Describe the components of a fuzzy system and the role of each, using the air-conditioner example.

Crisp input (room temperature, target) → fuzzifier (membership functions turn it into degrees such as cold, warm, hot) → inference (applies the fuzzy rules built from the fuzzy sets, e.g. IF temperature is hot AND target is warm THEN command is cool, taken from the expert-written associative matrix) → defuzzifier (turns the fuzzy conclusion into one value) → crisp output (heat, cool or no change, or a speed).

W5. Write the triangular and trapezoidal membership functions and compute triangle (20, 25, 30) at 23 and trapezoid (20, 25, 30, 35) at 22, 28 and 33.

Triangular (a, m, b): 0 for x ≤ a or x ≥ b; (x − a)/(m − a) for a < x ≤ m; (b − x)/(b − m) for m < x < b. Trapezoidal (a, b, c, d): (x − a)/(b − a) on a to b; 1 on b to c; (d − x)/(d − c) on c to d; else 0. Triangle at 23: 3/5 = 0.6. Trapezoid at 22: 2/5 = 0.4; at 28: 1; at 33: 2/5 = 0.4.

W6. Explain Mamdani inference step by step (antecedent, implication, aggregation), using service = 3 and food = 8 for 'service is excellent OR food is delicious'.

1) Fuzzify inputs and evaluate the antecedent: excellent(3) = 0.0, delicious(8) = 0.7; OR = max = 0.7 (the rule strength). 2) Implication: use the minimum to truncate the consequent (tip is generous) at 0.7 (the product would scale it instead). 3) Aggregation: combine the clipped curves of all rules with max (or sum, probabilistic sum) into one fuzzy output; then defuzzify (centroid), giving e.g. tip = 16.7 %.

W7. Defuzzify a combined output with degrees 0.4 at speeds 40, 50, 60 and 0.6 at speeds 70, 80, 90, 100 using the centroid. Compare with Sugeno constants 50 and 80 at strengths 0.4 and 0.6.

Top = 40×0.4 + 50×0.4 + 60×0.4 + 70×0.6 + 80×0.6 + 90×0.6 + 100×0.6 = 264; bottom = 3×0.4 + 4×0.6 = 3.6; output 73.3. Sugeno: (0.4×50 + 0.6×80)/(0.4 + 0.6) = 68. Mamdani needs the output curves; Sugeno only needs constants.

W8. For the heater controller at 35 °F and 1 °F/hr, four rules fire with strengths 0.6 (FAST), 0.4 (SLOW), 0.4 (FAST) and 0.8 (MEDIUM). Find the crisp motor speed given peaks FAST 800, MEDIUM 500, SLOW 300.

y = (0.6×800 + 0.4×300 + 0.4×800 + 0.8×500)/(0.6 + 0.4 + 0.4 + 0.8) = (480 + 120 + 320 + 400)/2.2 = 1320/2.2 = 600 R/M. Rule strengths are the minimum of the temperature and change-rate degrees.
