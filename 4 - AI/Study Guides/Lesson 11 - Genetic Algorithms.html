Genetic Algorithms

Covers: optimisation problems (decision variables, objective function, constraints), why derivatives and hill climbing fail, meta-heuristic (population-based) search, genetic algorithm terminology, encoding, the GA loop, roulette-wheel and tournament selection, crossover, mutation, replacement and termination, worked iterations (x squared and knapsack), special cases (travelling salesman with random keys, real-coded and multi-objective problems) and global, local and near optima. No prior lecture attendance needed.

Learning outcomes

  • State an optimisation problem (decision variables, objective function, constraints) and explain why the derivative method and hill climbing are not enough
  • Explain meta-heuristic (population-based) optimisation and where genetic algorithms fit
  • Use GA vocabulary: population, chromosome, gene, allele, phenotype, fitness, generation; encode and decode binary chromosomes
  • Perform roulette-wheel and tournament selection, crossover and mutation by hand, and run a full generation
  • List GA termination conditions; explain global, local and near optima
  • Handle special cases: travelling salesman (random keys), real-coded GAs, knapsack, multi-objective problems

1. The optimisation problem (slides, peer notes)

Optimisation = finding the best answer among many possible answers. Every optimisation problem has three parts:

PartMeaningExample (study timetable)
Decision variablesWhat we can change / need to optimiseHours to study each subject
Objective functionThe score to make as small (or big) as possible: min f(x)Total marks (maximise)
ConstraintsLimits we must obey: hard (equality) gi(x) = ci, i = 1..n; inequality hj(x) ≥ dj, j = 1..mOnly 10 hours in a day

Optimisation problems can be approximated by a mathematical function, the objective function. Example used in the lessons: Max z = x², 0 ≤ x < 32, x a whole number (the answer is obviously 31, so we can check that a method works). In a real problem you write your own f(x) for profit, distance or cost; the method stays the same.

Optimisation is also an ML job (Lesson 5): "finding the best possible solution for one or more objectives", e.g. minimise cost and emissions.

2. The classical way and why it fails (slides)

  • If we know the first derivative f′(x), we find the optimum where f′(x*) = 0 (the ground is flat at a hilltop or valley). The second derivative tells which: f″(x*) < 0: maxima; f″(x*) > 0: minima.
  • Example: f(x) = 9 − (x − 3)²: f(2) = 8, f(3) = 9, f(4) = 8: the top is at x = 3. (Slope = how steep the ground is: uphill positive, downhill negative, flat top 0.)
  • Unfortunately, for many practical problems this is intractable, or we cannot compute f′(x) (there is no neat formula). An alternative: generate a set of feasible solutions X = {x1, …, xn}, evaluate each, f(x1), …, f(xn), take min{f(x1), …, f(xn)} and "hope this is the true solution" (the slide asks: what could possibly go wrong?).

Analogy: finding the highest hill in thick fog with no map. You can only check how high you are where you stand.

3. Gradient descent (hill climbing) (slides)

Start with an initial feasible solution x1. Consider small changes Δx1, Δx2, Δx3 and take the better one: x2 = min{x1+Δx1, x1+Δx2, x1+Δx3} (compared by f). Continue, producing the sequence f(x1) > f(x2) > … > f(xk); terminate when no lesser local solution is found. It is an iterative method generating a sequence of minimisers, the most recent being the best found so far (the same idea as the gradient descent of Lesson 5).

Unfortunately it does not work for many problems and the results are sensitive to initial conditions: a walker that starts on the left rolls into the left valley and stops at a local minimum, never discovering the deeper valley on the right.

4. Meta-heuristic optimisation (slides)

Another strategy: start with a population of feasible solutions, generate new solutions in the neighbourhood of the previous candidates, and continue until no further improvements can be found; the optimal solution is taken as the best in the population at convergence.

Scope of meta-heuristics (slides): there are a large number of optimisation algorithms based on meta-heuristics. The slide's map groups them into population-based methods (evolutionary algorithms: genetic algorithm, genetic programming, evolutionary programming, differential evolution, evolution strategy; also particle swarm optimisation, ant colony optimisation, estimation-of-distribution algorithms) and trajectory-based / local-search methods (simulated annealing, tabu search, GRASP, iterated and variable-neighbourhood local search). The lecture presents one: the genetic algorithm, based on evolutionary principles. (Swarm intelligence is Lesson 12.)

5. Genetic algorithms: the idea and the words

Darwin's idea: the fittest survive and have children; children inherit good traits; over generations the group gets better. A GA does this with answers instead of animals: fit solutions combine to make fitter ones. A GA is an optimisation method that uses selection, crossover and mutation to find better solutions. (Evolutionary computing is the wider family; combinatorial optimisation, finding the best from a huge number of combinations such as shortest route, scheduling, travelling salesman, resource allocation and job assignment, is a typical use.) Analogy: a farmer saving seeds from the best plants every year; or many hikers in fog, where the higher ones "have children" that start near the good spots.

For marks, say how you are using the words (e.g. "chromosome 10010 stands for the solution x = 18").

Encoding: how an answer becomes a chromosome

Holland's genetic algorithm uses binary strings (e.g. 10111010101). Each position has a place value from the right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, …; add the place values where the bit is 1. 10010 = 16 + 2 = 18; 00111 = 4 + 2 + 1 = 7; 10000 = 16; 00010 = 2; 01101 = 8 + 4 + 1 = 13. With n bits you can write 2n numbers (5 bits: 32 numbers, 0 to 31).

Fitness must give a higher score to a better answer. For x² fitness = x². For shortest route we want small distance, so use fitness = 1 / total distance (100 km → 0.01, 80 km → 0.0125).

6. The GA loop

  1. Decide the population size N (too small: too little variety, gets stuck; too large: each generation is slow).
  2. Decide the chromosome structure (encoding).
  3. Decide the fitness; create the random initial population (generation 0) and evaluate each solution.
  4. Loop: selection (choose good parents: roulette wheel or binary tournament) → crossover (make children) → mutation (randomly change genes) → more children to make? repeat.
  5. Make the new population (weak individuals eliminated, population size kept constant); stopping criteria met? If not, repeat.

Why each step exists: selection pushes the group towards good answers (without it the search is random); crossover combines good parts of two good answers; mutation adds fresh variety so the group does not get stuck. (Probability of "being a parent" depends on fitness; parents can create the next generation by reproduction (copy a string unmodified), crossover, mutation or a combination.)

7. Selection: choosing the parents (slides)

Selection chooses two strings for breeding with a probability proportionate to their relative fitness: roulette wheel selection: each string receives a number of slots on a wheel equal to its fitness; the wheel is spun to generate two (unique) samples.

P(i) = f(i) / Ff(i) = fitness of string i; F = total fitness = f(1) + … + f(N).

8. Crossover (slides)

Given two strings taken from the population by selection, select a site between genes at random (uniform probability), then swap the tails to produce two offspring. Slide example: parents 11000000 and 11001001 with the cut after the 5th gene give offspring 11000001 and 11001000. Analogy: two answer sheets: first half from student 1, second half from student 2.

  • 4 steps: take two parents; choose a cut point (5 genes: 4 possible cut points); split both; Child 1 = front of P1 + back of P2, Child 2 = front of P2 + back of P1.
  • Worked: D = 10|010 (18) and B = 00|111 (7), cut after 2: Child 1 = 10111 = 23 (fitness 529), Child 2 = 00010 = 2 (fitness 4). Child 1 beats both parents (324 and 49): D's strong front (the 16) joined B's strong back.
  • The cut point matters: P1 = 11001 (25) and P2 = 00110 (6): cut after gene 1 gives 22 and 9 (484, 81); after 2: 30 and 1 (900, 1); after 3: 26 and 5; after 4: 24 and 7. No cut is always best; it is chosen at random.
  • Limits: crossover only reshuffles genes that already exist (if every parent has 0 in the last gene, every child does too); identical fronts give copies; it can break some problems (TSP).

9. Mutation (slides)

Finally we test each gene in each offspring for mutation: with a very small probability pm we randomly select a new allele value for that gene (binary: flip 0 ↔ 1). Slide example: 11000001 with the 4th gene flipped becomes 11010001; the other offspring 11001000 is unchanged. We then compute the fitness of both offspring (112 and 96 in the slide) and insert them into the population. Analogy: a typing mistake while copying a recipe: usually worse, once in a while a new favourite.

Which gene you flip matters (10111 = 23, fitness 529)

Mutation can help or hurt; flipping a high-value gene changes the answer a lot; a bad mutation is not a disaster because the weak child scores low and selection rarely picks it as a parent. Why we really need mutation: in the group 10010, 10100, 11000, 10000 every last gene is 0, so crossover can never create a 1 there and the group can reach at most 11110 = 30; only mutation can create the new gene value to reach 11111 = 31. Too little mutation: the group loses variety and gets stuck; too much: good answers are destroyed and the GA becomes a random guess. For real-coded genes mutation nudges the value (4.62 → 4.80) instead of flipping.

10. Replacement, termination and a full round

Holland's algorithm, last step (slides): eliminate (kill off) the weakest individuals so the population size stays constant. Slide example: the five strings (fitness 20, 36, 16, 84, 124) plus the two offspring (112, 96); strike out 20 and 16 leaves 36, 84, 124, 112, 96.

Termination of a GA (slides)The generational process repeats until a termination condition is reached. Common conditions: a solution is found that satisfies minimum criteria; a fixed number of generations is reached; the allocated budget (computation time / money) is reached; the highest-ranking solution's fitness has reached a plateau so successive iterations no longer produce better results; manual inspection; or any combination of these. (Handwritten notes: maximum generations, desired fitness achieved, no improvement for several generations.)

Fitness is low at the start, rises as better answers appear, then flattens: the GA has converged to a good solution (like learning a new sport: fast early progress, tiny gains later).

Travelling salesman problem (TSP) and random keys (slides, Exercise 02)

Visit every city once, in the order that minimises total distance. A chromosome is an order of cities, e.g. (4 2 1 5 3) = path 3 → 2 → 5 → 1 → 4 (position i holds the rank of city i; the slide's own convention). One-point crossover is difficult: parents (1 4 3 2 5) and (2 4 5 3 1) give children (1 4 3 3 1) and (2 4 5 1 4): cities repeat and others are missing, so these are not valid routes (peer example: P1 = 143|652, P2 = 321|645 gives 143645: city 4 twice, city 2 missing).

Random key method (the alternative): assign each city a random number drawn uniformly from (0, 1) and decode in ascending order (descending could be used too). Slide example: keys 0.42, 0.06, 0.38, 0.48, 0.81 for cities 1 to 5 decode as ranks 3, 1, 2, 4, 5, i.e. path 2 → 3 → 1 → 4 → 5 (smallest key first). Each city has exactly one key, so every route is valid.

  • Crossover operator: different methods can be used, e.g. parameterised uniform crossover: select two parents randomly, inheriting each gene from parent 1 with probability p and from parent 2 with probability 1 − p. Slide table: Parent 1 = 0.56, 0.81, 0.22, 0.7, 0.86; Parent 2 = 0.29, 0.49, 0.98, 0.12, 0.32; Offspring = 0.59 (printed; parent 1 has 0.56, so this looks like a typo), 0.49, 0.22, 0.12, 0.86 (genes 2 and 4 from parent 2, the others from parent 1).
  • Mutation operator (immigration): replace the worst set with a newly created set of candidates generated at random with the random key method. It avoids premature convergence.
  • Peer example: P1 keys 0.97, 0.68, 0.72, 0.15, 0.48, 0.32 → sorted 0.15 (city 4), 0.32 (6), 0.48 (5), 0.68 (2), 0.72 (3), 0.97 (1): route 4, 6, 5, 2, 3, 1. P2 keys 0.62, 0.38, 0.87, 0.92, 0.16, 0.32 → route 5, 6, 2, 1, 3, 4. Cut after 3 keys: C1 keys 0.97, 0.68, 0.72, 0.92, 0.16, 0.32 → route 5, 6, 2, 3, 4, 1; C2 keys 0.62, 0.38, 0.87, 0.15, 0.48, 0.32 → route 4, 6, 2, 5, 1, 3: both valid. Check: keys for cities 1–4 = 0.9, 0.2, 0.5, 0.1 give route 4, 2, 3, 1.
  • The slide exercise for 7 cities asks you to formulate a chromosome, hand-execute one iteration with 10 chromosomes and say what problems you met (invalid routes) and how to solve them (random keys).

Real-coded GA, combinatorial and multi-objective problems

  • Real-coded GA: genes are real numbers, e.g. (0.25, 1.73, 4.62, 2.15), for naturally continuous variables (speed, length, temperature).
  • Multi-objective optimisation (slides): more than one objective function at once, e.g. minimise cost and CO2 emissions. The slide's example: minimise f1(x) = x1² − x1x2 + x2² − x1 − x2 + 1 and f2(x) = x1² + x1x2 + x2² + 2x1 + 4x2 + 4; "is it possible to solve this using GA? Give it a try." Here the goals conflict: f1 is smallest (0) at x = (1, 1), where f2 = 13; f2 is smallest (0) at x = (0, −2), where f1 = 7. So there is no single best answer, only good trade-offs (analogy: a phone that is cheap and has a great camera). A GA can handle it because it only needs fitness values: e.g. combine the goals into one score such as f1 + f2, or keep a population of trade-off solutions.
  • Slide Exercise 02 (The Spinning Wheel amusement park): several attractions, each with a survey satisfaction level and a waiting time; visitors enter until 3 PM, the park closes 6 PM; find an optimal visiting plan maximising satisfaction within the remaining time. Suggested answer: yes, a GA is suitable: it is a combinatorial planning problem (an order / subset of attractions) with no neat derivative; chromosome = random keys giving the visiting order (or a 0/1 gene per attraction); fitness = total satisfaction of the attractions that fit before closing time (plus a penalty for exceeding it); the usual selection, crossover and mutation apply.

12. Global, local and near optima; escaping local optima

  • Global optimum: the best solution among all possible. Local optimum: better than its neighbours but not the best overall. Near optimum: very close to the global optimum but not exactly the best. Analogy: hiking in fog: you stop on a small hill, but Everest is somewhere else.
  • How a GA escapes a small hill: 3-bit chromosomes with fitness x = 0:1, 1:3, 2:6, 3:3, 4:2, 5:5, 6:9, 7:4 (small hill at x = 2, highest peak at x = 6). A group stuck near the hill, 001, 010, 011, all start with 0; crossover can never create a first gene 1, but mutation flips the first gene of 010 to get 110 = 6 (fitness 9). Also, a GA keeps a whole population spread over the landscape (hill climbing keeps one point) and roulette selection gives weaker answers a small chance, which helps find the global optimum. A GA is therefore not guaranteed to find the best, but it finds very good answers when we cannot calculate them ("hope it is the true solution", slide).

Written questions

W1. Explain why the derivative method and hill climbing are not enough for many optimisation problems, and how a population-based meta-heuristic helps.

Calculus needs f′(x) (solve f′(x*) = 0 and check the sign of f″) but for many practical problems it is intractable or cannot be computed. Hill climbing starts at one feasible solution, tries small changes and keeps the best until no lesser neighbour exists, but it is sensitive to initial conditions and can stop in a local minimum. A population-based meta-heuristic starts with many feasible solutions spread over the search space, generates new solutions in their neighbourhoods and repeats until no improvement; the best in the population at convergence is the answer, so one bad starting point does not trap the search.

W2. Define population, chromosome, gene, allele, fitness and generation, and the three genetic operators.

Population: a group of candidate solutions; chromosome: one solution as a string; gene: one position of the string; allele: a value a gene can take (0 or 1); fitness: the objective function value (higher means better); generation: one cycle of the algorithm. Operators: selection (choose parents in proportion to fitness), crossover (swap tails of two parents at a random cut), mutation (randomly flip a gene with a very small probability).

W3. For fitness 20, 36, 16, 84, 124, compute the roulette probabilities and explain how a computer selects a parent.

Total 280: 7.1 %, 12.9 %, 5.7 %, 30.0 %, 44.3 %. Running totals 0.071, 0.200, 0.257, 0.557, 1.000. Pick a random r in (0, 1) and choose the string whose interval contains r (r = 0.30 picks the 84; r = 0.75 picks the 124). Weak strings keep a small chance, preserving variety.

W4. Parents 11001 and 00110 are crossed after gene 2, and then gene 4 of the first child is mutated. Show the children, the mutated child and the x values (fitness = x²).

Cut after gene 2: 11|001 and 00|110 give child 1 = 11110 (30, fitness 900) and child 2 = 00001 (1, fitness 1). Mutating gene 4 of child 1 (1 to 0) gives 11100 (28, fitness 784), slightly worse; flipping a high-value gene changes the answer a lot.

W5. Perform one generation for Max x² with 01101, 11000, 01000, 10011 using mating pool A, B, B, D with crossovers A×B after gene 4 and B×D after gene 2 and no mutation. Compare average fitness.

Initial fitness 169, 576, 64, 361 (total 1170, average 292.5). Crossover: 01101 × 11000 after 4 gives 01100 (12) and 11001 (25); 11000 × 10011 after 2 gives 11011 (27) and 10000 (16). New fitness 144, 625, 729, 256: total 1754, average 438.5, best 729 (up from 576): the population improved.

W6. Formulate the knapsack exercise (weights 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 12 kg; values 100, 300, 200, 500, 150, 400; capacity 15 kg) as a GA: chromosome, fitness, and one iteration.

Chromosome: 6 bits (1 = pack the block). Fitness: total value if total weight ≤ 15, else 0. Example population A 110100 (900), B 010001 (700), C 001010 (350), D 100011 (22 kg, so 0). Select A and C; crossover after gene 3 gives 110010 (550) and 001100 (700); mutate gene 2 of 001100 to get 011100: blocks 3, 5, 7 kg, weight 15, value 1000, the optimum.

W7. Why does normal crossover fail for the travelling salesman problem, and how does the random key method fix it? Decode keys 0.42, 0.06, 0.38, 0.48, 0.81.

Swapping tails of two permutations can repeat some cities and omit others (parents (1 4 3 2 5), (2 4 5 3 1) give (1 4 3 3 1)). Random keys give every city one random number in (0, 1); sort ascending to get the order, so any crossover (e.g. parameterised uniform) or mutation (immigration) on the keys always decodes to a valid route. The keys decode to the path 2 → 3 → 1 → 4 → 5.

W8. Explain global, local and near optima and how a GA can escape a local optimum. List the termination conditions.

Global optimum: best among all solutions; local: better than neighbours but not overall; near: very close to the global optimum. A GA keeps a spread-out population, roulette selection gives weak answers a small chance, and mutation creates new gene values (e.g. flipping the first gene of 010 gives 110 = x of 6, the highest peak). Termination: a solution meets minimum criteria, fixed number of generations, budget reached, fitness plateau, manual inspection or any combination.
