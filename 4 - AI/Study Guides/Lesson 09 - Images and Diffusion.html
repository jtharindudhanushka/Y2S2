2. Colourising photos as a generation task (from the course: assignment)

The Lab colour space

RGB stores colour as red, green, blue. Lab splits a colour differently: L = lightness (this is the grayscale picture), and a, b = two channels that carry the colour. So colourising becomes:

Taskinput = L, shape 1 × 128 × 128; target = (a, b), shape 2 × 128 × 128 (channels first, as in PyTorch). Predict a and b, put L, a, b back together and convert to RGB.

3. The U-Net (from the course: assignment code)

An autoencoder squeezes its input into a compact summary (encoder) and rebuilds an output from it (decoder). A U-Net is an autoencoder with skip connections: shortcuts that hand the detailed (high-resolution) encoder features straight to the matching decoder stage. Analogy: summarise and redraw, with sticky notes of fine detail handed to the artist so nothing is forgotten. The name comes from the U shape of the diagram.

Reading the code

  • DoubleConv(in, out): Conv2d 3×3 with padding 1 (keeps the size) → BatchNorm2d → ReLU, twice. BatchNorm keeps the numbers in a steady range so training is stable (a standard technique, not in the lecture slides).
  • Encoder: four DoubleConv blocks with 64, 128, 256, 512 channels. After each block MaxPool2d(2) halves height and width (Lesson 7) while the channel count doubles.
  • Bottleneck: DoubleConv with 1024 channels at the smallest size: the compact summary.
  • Decoder: each stage is ConvTranspose2d(…, 2, stride 2) (a learned up-sampling that doubles height and width and halves the channels) followed by torch.cat with the matching encoder output (the skip connection: channels are joined, which is why a decoder block takes twice the channels, e.g. 512 + 512 = 1024) and a DoubleConv.
  • Output: a 1×1 convolution to 2 channels (a and b) then tanh, which squashes to −1 to 1 to match the normalised targets.

The notebook's own check: a random input of shape (1, 1, 128, 128) gives an output of shape (1, 2, 128, 128). Counting the learnable weights and biases (including BatchNorm's two numbers per channel) gives about 31 million parameters. Every number, like the filters of Lesson 7, is learned by backpropagation.

An honest limit (from the peer notes)One grayscale photo can match several plausible colourings (a jacket could be red or blue), so the network learns plausible colours, not the true historical ones. MSE also tends to average uncertain colours, so results often look muted. Also, a model trained on tiny 32×32 CIFAR-10 pictures resized to 128×128 sees a limited range of scenes, so an old family portrait is a stretch.

5. The big picture: why U-Net suits "image in, image out"

  • A classification CNN (Lesson 7) shrinks an image to a few class scores. Colourising needs a full image out, so the decoder must grow the maps back.
  • The bottleneck forces the model to learn a compact understanding of the scene (this is sky, this is a face), while the skip connections preserve edges and fine detail, so the output lines up exactly with the input pixels.
  • The same shape is used for many "image to image" jobs (restoration, segmentation) and, as section 6 explains, as the denoising network inside diffusion models.

6. Background: how diffusion, GANs and VAEs generate images (not from your course materials)

BackgroundThe slide names Stable Diffusion, VAEs and GANs but your folder contains no explanation of them. This section is a short standard introduction, not from the lecture slides; learn the course facts above first.

Diffusion models (the idea behind Stable Diffusion)

  1. Forward process (add noise): take a real image and add a little random noise, again and again, until only noise is left. This needs no learning.
  2. Reverse process (remove noise): train a neural network (very often a U-Net) to look at a noisy image and predict the noise in it. Training is just MSE between the predicted noise and the noise that was actually added.
  3. Generate: start from pure random noise and apply the trained network many times, removing a little noise each step, until a clean, new image appears. A text prompt can guide each step (this is how "AI art" from a prompt works).

Tiny arithmetic example: one pixel with true value 0.8 and a random noise value 0.5. With a noise level where the kept fraction of the image is 0.8 and the noise fraction is 0.6 (0.8² + 0.6² = 1), the noisy pixel is 0.8 × 0.8 + 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.64 + 0.30 = 0.94; later steps keep less image and more noise. Learning to undo this is what the U-Net is trained for.

GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks)

Two networks compete: a generator turns random numbers into fake images and a discriminator tries to tell real from fake. The discriminator is rewarded for catching fakes, the generator for fooling it; as both improve, the fakes become realistic. (This is the discriminative / generative pairing of Lesson 8 put in one system.)

VAEs (Variational Autoencoders)

A VAE is an autoencoder (section 3) whose encoder summarises an image as a small cloud of numbers (a latent description). To generate, pick a new random point in that space and let the decoder turn it into a new image. VAEs tend to be stable but a little blurry.

Written questions

W1. Describe how the colourisation assignment frames the problem in the Lab colour space, including the shapes of the input and target.

Lab splits colour into L (lightness, the grayscale picture) and a, b (colour). The network input is L with shape 1×128×128; the target is a and b with shape 2×128×128. Predict a and b, combine with L and convert Lab to RGB. Training images are resized to 128×128, converted RGB→Lab and scaled (L/50 − 1, ab/128); the same scaling is reversed when rebuilding the colour image.

W2. Explain the U-Net used in the assignment: encoder, bottleneck, decoder, skip connections and output layer, with the shapes.

Encoder: DoubleConv blocks with 64, 128, 256, 512 channels, with max-pooling halving the size (128, 64, 32, 16). Bottleneck: 1024 channels at 8×8. Decoder: ConvTranspose2d doubles the size and halves channels, then the matching encoder output is concatenated (skip connection, so a block takes 512 + 512 = 1024 channels) and a DoubleConv follows, returning to 64 channels at 128×128. Output: 1×1 convolution to 2 channels with tanh, shape 2×128×128. Skip connections carry fine detail so the output lines up with the input.

W3. Describe the training procedure and loss for the colouriser and how you would apply it to an old black-and-white photograph.

MSE between predicted and true a, b, optimised with Adam (lr 0.001) for 15–20 epochs (18 used); for each batch zero gradients, forward pass, MSE, backward (backpropagation), optimiser step; save a checkpoint each epoch and plot the loss. For a photo: convert to grayscale, resize to 128×128, use as L (scaled), predict a and b, recombine with L, convert to RGB and show the grayscale input, the prediction and the restored colour image.

W4. Give two limits of this colouriser.

One grayscale image can match several plausible colourings (a jacket could be red or blue), so the output is plausible rather than true; MSE averages uncertain colours so results look muted. Also the training images are tiny CIFAR-10 pictures, so unusual scenes such as old portraits are harder.

W5. (Background) Explain in your own words how a diffusion model generates an image.

Forward process: add a little random noise to real images again and again until only noise remains. Training: a neural network (often a U-Net) learns to predict the noise in a noisy image (MSE on the noise). Generation: start from pure random noise and repeatedly apply the network to remove a little noise each step until a clean new image appears; a text prompt can guide the steps (Stable Diffusion).

W6. (Background) Compare GANs and VAEs as image generators.

GAN: a generator turns random numbers into fake images and a discriminator tries to tell real from fake; they compete and both improve, giving sharp images but hard training. VAE: an autoencoder whose encoder describes an image as a random latent cloud; generation samples a new point and decodes it; stable but often blurry.
