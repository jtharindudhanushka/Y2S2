1. What is a transducer?

A transducer converts energy (or a physical quantity) from one form to another. Transducers sit at the boundary of an embedded system: they are how it touches the real world. They are grouped by the direction of conversion:

Figure 1. Sensors capture the state of the physical world as inputs; actuators change it.
SensorConverts a physical quantity into an electrical signal (usually a voltage). Examples: LDR, LM35, reed switch, PIR, accelerometer.
ActuatorConverts electrical energy into another form: light (LED, bulb), motion (motor, solenoid), heat/cold (Peltier), sound (buzzer).

BothSound, soil moisture, gas/smoke modules have an Aout (analog) pin and a Dout (digital) pin.

How a module gives both Aout and Dout

Figure 2. Inside an "analog/digital" module: a comparator (often an LM393 chip) compares the sensor voltage with a threshold you set with the small blue screw pot.

Use Dout when you only need "wet/dry", "loud/quiet", "gas/no gas" and want to set the threshold with a screwdriver. Use Aout when the program needs the level or must choose the threshold itself. Module pins are usually labelled VCC (+5 V), GND, Aout, Dout.

3. The digital sensors

Reed switch

Two thin metal contacts sealed in a glass tube with inert gas. Normally open; a magnet nearby magnetises the contacts so they attract and close. It is simply a switch operated by a magnet, so wire it exactly like a pushbutton: one side to the pin with a pull-up, the other side to GND.

Use: door/window alarms (magnet on the door, reed on the frame), position sensing, counting wheel turns.

Hall effect sensor (AH1815)

A semiconductor chip that produces a small voltage when a magnetic field passes through it (the Hall effect); built-in electronics turn this into a clean on/off output. The AH1815 is omnipolar (reacts to a north or south pole), runs on 2.5–5.5 V, and has an open-drain output: with a magnet the output is pulled LOW, otherwise it is "off" (floating). So it needs a pull-up resistor (internal pull-up or 10 kΩ to 5 V). No moving parts, so it is faster and lasts longer than a reed switch.

Pins (flat face towards you): VDD, GND, OUTPUT — check the datasheet drawing for your package.

Figure 3. Reed switch with pull-up (same circuit for an open-drain Hall sensor: its output replaces the switch).
Open-drain / open-collector, in plain words The output can only connect the line to GND (LOW) or let go (nothing). It cannot push the line HIGH by itself, so a pull-up resistor provides the HIGH. It lets the sensor work with any logic voltage.

PIR sensor (motion)

Pyroelectric InfraRed. Warm bodies give off infrared. A pyroelectric element produces a voltage when the IR falling on it changes; a white Fresnel lens splits the view into zones, so a person moving across zones causes changes. It detects movement, not presence: a person standing still is not seen. The module output goes HIGH (about 3.3 V, still read as HIGH) for a few seconds after motion. Modules have two trimmers (sensitivity and on-time) and need about 30–60 s to settle after power-up. Pins: +5 V, Dout, GND.

Flame sensor

An IR photodiode/phototransistor tuned to the infrared emitted by a fire, with a comparator giving a digital output (often LOW = flame, check your module's LED). Use: fire alarms (the slide's example of a microcontroller system with binary inputs and outputs).

5. Actuators, and why a pin cannot drive them directly

An I/O pin can supply only 5 V and about 20 mA (40 mA absolute maximum, and the whole chip about 200 mA). An LED (15 mA) is fine. But:

ActuatorConverts toNeeds
RGB LEDred, green, blue light; mixing gives other coloursFine from pins: three LEDs in one package, one resistor and one pin each (PWM pins to mix shades, Lesson 8). Common cathode: shared leg to GND, pin HIGH = on. Common anode: shared leg to 5 V, pin LOW = on.
Incandescent bulblight and heatHigher voltage and current: transistor or relay.
DC motorcircular motionEven small ones draw 100–500 mA, and more when starting: transistor/MOSFET + flyback diode, often its own supply.
Solenoidstraight-line motion (a plunger)Higher voltage and current: MOSFET + flyback diode.
Thermoelectric (Peltier)heat on one face, cold on the otherAmps of current: MOSFET or relay, separate supply.
The rule The pin only controls a switch; a separate, stronger supply powers the load. The switch is a BJT transistor, a MOSFET, or a relay. The grounds of the two supplies must be joined, or the control signal has no reference.

6. Driving a load with a BJT (BC547)

Figure 4. Low-side switch: the load sits between +V and the collector; the emitter goes to GND.

How a BJT works: it has three legs, Base, Collector, Emitter. A small current into the base allows a much bigger current from collector to emitter. The ratio is the current gain hFE (110–800 for BC547): IC = hFE × IB. As a switch we give more base current than needed, so the transistor is fully on (saturated: only about 0.2 V across C–E) or fully off (no base current).

Water picture: the base is a small tap controlling a big valve.

Base resistor calculation 1. Load current IC, e.g. relay coil 70 mA.  2. Minimum base current = IC / hFE(min) = 70 / 110 = 0.64 mA.  3. Multiply by about 5 for hard saturation: IB ≈ 3.2 mA.  4. RB = (Vpin − VBE) / IB = (5 − 0.7) / 0.0032 = 1.3 kΩ → use 1 kΩ (gives 4.3 mA, still safe for the pin).

BC547 limits: IC max 100 mA, VCE max 45 V. A bigger motor needs a bigger transistor or a MOSFET. Pins (flat face towards you): C, B, E.

Why "low side"? With an NPN the emitter must be at GND so the 5 V pin can push current into the base. The load goes on the "high" (+V) side.

7. Driving a load with a MOSFET (2N7000)

Figure 5. N-MOSFET as a low-side switch.

How a MOSFET works: three legs, Gate, Drain, Source. It is controlled by voltage, not current: when the gate is more than the threshold voltage (0.8–3 V for 2N7000) above the source, a channel opens and current flows from drain to source. The gate is insulated, so it draws almost no current (only a tiny charge when switching).

The 10 kΩ pull-down keeps the gate at 0 V (MOSFET off) while the microcontroller is resetting and its pins are still floating inputs. Otherwise the motor might twitch at power-up.

2N7000 limits: about 200 mA continuous, 60 V. For motors of several amps use a "logic-level" power MOSFET (one fully on at VGS = 5 V). P-MOSFET: the opposite type, used on the high (+V) side; gate pulled LOW to turn on.

8. The flyback diode: why every motor and relay needs one

Figure 6. A coil (motor, relay, solenoid) stores energy in its magnetic field and resists any sudden change of current.

Why it happens: current through a coil cannot stop instantly. When the transistor suddenly opens, the coil forces the current to keep going by producing a very high voltage across the transistor. The flyback (freewheeling) diode placed across the coil, cathode to +V, gives that current a loop to circulate in until it dies out. It is "reverse-biased" (off) while the load runs normally. The 1N4001 (1 A, 50 V, ~0.7–1.1 V drop) is the standard choice. Other diode uses (web resource): rectifying AC to DC, protecting a circuit from a reversed battery, and LEDs themselves.

9. Switching with a relay

Figure 7. A relay: an electromagnet that moves a switch. COM = common, NO = normally open, NC = normally closed.

How it works: current through the coil makes a magnetic field that pulls the moving contact from NC over to NO. Coil off: a spring pulls it back. You hear a click.

Why use one: the coil circuit and the contact circuit are electrically isolated, so 5 V logic can switch 230 V AC or large DC loads. The contact rating (e.g. 10 A 250 VAC) is printed on the case.

Driving it: the coil needs more current than a pin gives, so use the BJT circuit of section 6 with the coil as the load and a flyback diode across it. A relay module already contains the transistor, flyback diode, an indicator LED and screw terminals: you connect VCC, GND and IN (to the pin).

Wiring a lamp: live (through a fuse) → COM, NO → lamp, lamp → neutral. Lamp lights only while the coil is on. Use NC instead for "on unless activated". Never build mains circuits on a breadboard.

Limits: slow (milliseconds), so not for PWM; contacts wear out.

10. Worked design questions (exam style)

A. Door alarm with a reed switch

Spec: when the door opens, an LED (or buzzer) turns on and stays on until reset. Design: magnet on the door, reed switch on the frame, wired from PD2 (pin 2) to GND with the internal pull-up. Door closed: magnet near, reed closed, pin LOW. Door open: reed opens, pin goes HIGH. LED on PB0 (pin 8) through 220 Ω.

void setup()
{
  pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP);       // reed switch
  pinMode(8, OUTPUT);             // alarm LED / buzzer
}
void loop()
{
  if (digitalRead(2) == HIGH)     // door opened
    digitalWrite(8, HIGH);        // latch on
}                                 // reset button clears it
#include <avr/io.h>
int main(void)
{
  DDRB  |=  (1<<PB0);
  DDRD  &= ~(1<<PD2);
  PORTD |=  (1<<PD2);            // pull-up
  while (1)
    if (PIND & (1<<PD2))         // HIGH = open
      PORTB |= (1<<PB0);         // alarm on
}

B. Fan that switches on when it is hot

Spec: LM35 on A0; a 6–9 V DC motor turns on at 30 °C and off at 28 °C. Design: motor powered from the 9 V battery through a 2N7000 (or BC547 if the motor draws under 100 mA) on PB1 (pin 9), flyback diode across the motor, 10 kΩ gate pull-down, grounds joined. Two thresholds (hysteresis, Lesson 5) stop it chattering at 29 °C. Pin 9 is also a PWM pin, so Lesson 8 can later control the speed (Exercise 6).

void setup() { pinMode(9, OUTPUT); }
void loop()
{
  float t = analogRead(A0) * 5.0 / 1024 * 100;   // LM35: degrees C
  if (t >= 30)      digitalWrite(9, HIGH);       // hot: fan on
  else if (t <= 28) digitalWrite(9, LOW);        // cool: fan off
  delay(500);
}

11. Build example: a motor driven by a BC547

This is component test 09 in the Component Tests folder (wiring and pictures there). Use the Lesson 3 section P method. What is new for actuators:

  1. Two supplies: the motor runs from the 9 V battery, never from the Uno 5 V pin. Join the battery − to the Uno GND rail. Never connect the battery + to the Uno 5 V or the breadboard + rail used by the Uno.
  2. Order of wiring: transistor first (check C, B, E), then 1 kΩ from the pin to the base, emitter to GND, motor between battery + and collector, diode across the motor (stripe to battery +). Connect the battery last, after checking.
  3. Test in steps: blink sketch on the pin with an LED in place of the motor first (proves the transistor), then the motor.
  4. Signs of a fault: Uno resets when the motor starts (motor on the Uno supply); motor always on (C and E swapped or transistor damaged); transistor hot (diode reversed, or motor current above 100 mA).
