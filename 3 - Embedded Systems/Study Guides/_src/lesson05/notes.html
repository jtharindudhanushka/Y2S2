1. What is a transducer?

  • Converts energy or physical quantities from one form to another.
  • Used at the boundary of an embedded system.
  • Classified by the direction of conversion: sensors (physical quantity → electrical signal, inputs) and actuators (electrical energy → other forms, outputs).

2. Sensors

Sensors capture the state of the physical world as inputs. They are digital (two discrete voltages, read through a digital input pin with PINx / digitalRead), analog (a continuous voltage, read with the ADC) or both.

3. Actuators

Actuators convert electrical energy into other forms and change the state of the physical world. Many need a higher voltage or more current than an I/O pin can deliver.

The ATmega328P pin limits: about 20 mA per pin recommended (40 mA absolute maximum), about 200 mA total for the chip. A small DC motor, a relay coil, a solenoid or a bulb will exceed this, so a driver is needed.

4. Driving a load with a BJT (BC547)

  • An NPN BJT is a current-controlled switch: a small base current lets a much larger current flow from collector to emitter. (PNP is the complementary type.)
  • Circuit: microcontroller pin → base resistor RB → base; emitter to GND (common with the microcontroller); load between the load supply +V and the collector.
  • Flyback diode across an inductive load (motor, relay, solenoid): cathode to +V, anode to the collector. When the transistor switches off, the coil's collapsing field drives current back through the diode instead of producing a high voltage spike that could destroy the transistor or the pin. The kit uses a 1N4001 rectifier diode (50 V reverse, 1 A forward, about 1.1 V forward drop).

Choosing RB

To saturate the transistor (fully on) force the base current to about one tenth of the collector current: IB ≈ IC / 10 (less than the gain hFE, which is 110 or more for a BC547). Then RB = (Vpin − VBE) / IB with Vpin = 5 V and VBE ≈ 0.7 V.

Worked exampleA 6 V motor draws 100 mA. IB = 100 mA / 10 = 10 mA. RB = (5 − 0.7) / 0.010 = 430 Ω, use 330 Ω or 470 Ω (the pin supplies 13 mA at 330 Ω, still within the pin limit). With 1 kΩ the base current is 4.3 mA, which saturates the transistor only up to about 43 mA. The BC547 handles up to 100 mA continuous (45 V VCEO); above that choose a power transistor.

5. Driving a load with a MOSFET (2N7000)

  • An N-channel enhancement MOSFET is voltage controlled: the gate draws practically no current in steady state. Terminals: gate G, drain D (load side), source S (to GND).
  • 2N7000: 60 V drain-source, 200 mA continuous, gate threshold 0.8 to 3 V, so a 5 V pin turns it on fully (logic-level). On-resistance a few ohms at VGS = 4.5 to 10 V.
  • Wiring: pin → gate (optionally through about 100 Ω to limit switching current); a 10 kΩ pull-down from gate to GND keeps it off while the pin floats (during reset or programming); load from +V to drain; source to GND; flyback diode across inductive loads.

6. Switching with a relay

  • A relay is an electromagnetic switch. A small coil current (from a transistor driver) closes the contacts of a separate, isolated circuit, so the microcontroller never touches the load voltage.
  • Contacts: COM (common), NO (normally open), NC (normally closed). The coil needs a transistor driver and a flyback diode. Relay modules include the transistor, diode and an indicator LED.
  • Use a relay (or a solid-state relay) for AC mains loads. Only with qualified supervision, correct isolation and enclosure.

7. Reading a digital sensor (code pattern)

// Hall switch AH1815 on PD2 (output low when a magnet is near), motor driver on PB0
DDRB |=  (1<<DDB0);                  // motor transistor base
DDRD &= ~(1<<DDD2);                  // sensor input
PORTD |= (1<<PORTD2);                 // pull-up for the open-drain output
while (1) {
    if ((PIND & (1<<PIND2)) == 0)     // magnet present
        PORTB |=  (1<<PORTB0);        // motor on
    else
        PORTB &= ~(1<<PORTB0);        // motor off
}
// Arduino
void setup() { pinMode(8, OUTPUT); pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP); }
void loop()  { digitalWrite(8, digitalRead(2) == LOW); }

8. Diode applications (reference reading)

  • Rectification (1N400X series): AC to DC; 1N4001 withstands 50 V reverse and 1 A.
  • Flyback / freewheeling protection across relay coils, motors and solenoids.
  • Reverse-polarity protection in series with a battery input.
  • LED indicators (light emitting diodes), signal clamping and logic.
Common mistakes1) Driving a motor straight from a pin. 2) Forgetting the flyback diode or fitting it backwards. 3) Not joining the grounds of the motor supply and the Arduino. 4) Using no pull-down on a MOSFET gate. 5) RB too large to saturate the BJT, which makes it hot. 6) Powering a motor from the 5 V pin of the Arduino (use a separate supply). 7) Connecting a mains circuit on a breadboard.

1. What is a transducer?

A transducer converts energy (or a physical quantity) from one form to another. Transducers sit at the boundary of an embedded system: they are how it touches the real world. They are grouped by the direction of conversion:

Figure 1. Sensors capture the state of the physical world as inputs; actuators change it.

ActuatorConverts electrical energy into another form: light (LED, bulb), motion (motor, solenoid), heat/cold (Peltier), sound (buzzer).

BothSound, soil moisture, gas/smoke modules have an Aout (analog) pin and a Dout (digital) pin.

How a module gives both Aout and Dout

Figure 2. Inside an "analog/digital" module: a comparator (often an LM393 chip) compares the sensor voltage with a threshold you set with the small blue screw pot.

Use Dout when you only need "wet/dry", "loud/quiet", "gas/no gas" and want to set the threshold with a screwdriver. Use Aout when the program needs the level or must choose the threshold itself. Module pins are usually labelled VCC (+5 V), GND, Aout, Dout.

3. The digital sensors

Reed switch

Two thin metal contacts sealed in a glass tube with inert gas. Normally open; a magnet nearby magnetises the contacts so they attract and close. It is simply a switch operated by a magnet, so wire it exactly like a pushbutton: one side to the pin with a pull-up, the other side to GND.

Use: door/window alarms (magnet on the door, reed on the frame), position sensing, counting wheel turns.

Hall effect sensor (AH1815)

A semiconductor chip that produces a small voltage when a magnetic field passes through it (the Hall effect); built-in electronics turn this into a clean on/off output. The AH1815 is omnipolar (reacts to a north or south pole), runs on 2.5–5.5 V, and has an open-drain output: with a magnet the output is pulled LOW, otherwise it is "off" (floating). So it needs a pull-up resistor (internal pull-up or 10 kΩ to 5 V). No moving parts, so it is faster and lasts longer than a reed switch.

Pins (flat face towards you): VDD, GND, OUTPUT — check the datasheet drawing for your package.

Figure 3. Reed switch with pull-up (same circuit for an open-drain Hall sensor: its output replaces the switch).
Open-drain / open-collector, in plain words The output can only connect the line to GND (LOW) or let go (nothing). It cannot push the line HIGH by itself, so a pull-up resistor provides the HIGH. It lets the sensor work with any logic voltage.

PIR sensor (motion)

Pyroelectric InfraRed. Warm bodies give off infrared. A pyroelectric element produces a voltage when the IR falling on it changes; a white Fresnel lens splits the view into zones, so a person moving across zones causes changes. It detects movement, not presence: a person standing still is not seen. The module output goes HIGH (about 3.3 V, still read as HIGH) for a few seconds after motion. Modules have two trimmers (sensitivity and on-time) and need about 30–60 s to settle after power-up. Pins: +5 V, Dout, GND.

Flame sensor

An IR photodiode/phototransistor tuned to the infrared emitted by a fire, with a comparator giving a digital output (often LOW = flame, check your module's LED). Use: fire alarms (the slide's example of a microcontroller system with binary inputs and outputs).

5. Actuators, and why a pin cannot drive them directly

An I/O pin can supply only 5 V and about 20 mA (40 mA absolute maximum, and the whole chip about 200 mA). An LED (15 mA) is fine. But:

The rule The pin only controls a switch; a separate, stronger supply powers the load. The switch is a BJT transistor, a MOSFET, or a relay. The grounds of the two supplies must be joined, or the control signal has no reference.

6. Driving a load with a BJT (BC547)

Figure 4. Low-side switch: the load sits between +V and the collector; the emitter goes to GND.

How a BJT works: it has three legs, Base, Collector, Emitter. A small current into the base allows a much bigger current from collector to emitter. The ratio is the current gain hFE (110–800 for BC547): IC = hFE × IB. As a switch we give more base current than needed, so the transistor is fully on (saturated: only about 0.2 V across C–E) or fully off (no base current).

Water picture: the base is a small tap controlling a big valve.

Base resistor calculation 1. Load current IC, e.g. relay coil 70 mA.  2. Minimum base current = IC / hFE(min) = 70 / 110 = 0.64 mA.  3. Multiply by about 5 for hard saturation: IB ≈ 3.2 mA.  4. RB = (Vpin − VBE) / IB = (5 − 0.7) / 0.0032 = 1.3 kΩ → use 1 kΩ (gives 4.3 mA, still safe for the pin).

BC547 limits: IC max 100 mA, VCE max 45 V. A bigger motor needs a bigger transistor or a MOSFET. Pins (flat face towards you): C, B, E.

Why "low side"? With an NPN the emitter must be at GND so the 5 V pin can push current into the base. The load goes on the "high" (+V) side.

7. Driving a load with a MOSFET (2N7000)

Figure 5. N-MOSFET as a low-side switch.

How a MOSFET works: three legs, Gate, Drain, Source. It is controlled by voltage, not current: when the gate is more than the threshold voltage (0.8–3 V for 2N7000) above the source, a channel opens and current flows from drain to source. The gate is insulated, so it draws almost no current (only a tiny charge when switching).

The 10 kΩ pull-down keeps the gate at 0 V (MOSFET off) while the microcontroller is resetting and its pins are still floating inputs. Otherwise the motor might twitch at power-up.

2N7000 limits: about 200 mA continuous, 60 V. For motors of several amps use a "logic-level" power MOSFET (one fully on at VGS = 5 V). P-MOSFET: the opposite type, used on the high (+V) side; gate pulled LOW to turn on.

8. The flyback diode: why every motor and relay needs one

Figure 6. A coil (motor, relay, solenoid) stores energy in its magnetic field and resists any sudden change of current.

Why it happens: current through a coil cannot stop instantly. When the transistor suddenly opens, the coil forces the current to keep going by producing a very high voltage across the transistor. The flyback (freewheeling) diode placed across the coil, cathode to +V, gives that current a loop to circulate in until it dies out. It is "reverse-biased" (off) while the load runs normally. The 1N4001 (1 A, 50 V, ~0.7–1.1 V drop) is the standard choice. Other diode uses (web resource): rectifying AC to DC, protecting a circuit from a reversed battery, and LEDs themselves.

9. Switching with a relay

Figure 7. A relay: an electromagnet that moves a switch. COM = common, NO = normally open, NC = normally closed.

How it works: current through the coil makes a magnetic field that pulls the moving contact from NC over to NO. Coil off: a spring pulls it back. You hear a click.

Why use one: the coil circuit and the contact circuit are electrically isolated, so 5 V logic can switch 230 V AC or large DC loads. The contact rating (e.g. 10 A 250 VAC) is printed on the case.

Driving it: the coil needs more current than a pin gives, so use the BJT circuit of section 6 with the coil as the load and a flyback diode across it. A relay module already contains the transistor, flyback diode, an indicator LED and screw terminals: you connect VCC, GND and IN (to the pin).

Wiring a lamp: live (through a fuse) → COM, NO → lamp, lamp → neutral. Lamp lights only while the coil is on. Use NC instead for "on unless activated". Never build mains circuits on a breadboard.

Limits: slow (milliseconds), so not for PWM; contacts wear out.

10. Worked design questions (exam style)

A. Door alarm with a reed switch

Spec: when the door opens, an LED (or buzzer) turns on and stays on until reset. Design: magnet on the door, reed switch on the frame, wired from PD2 (pin 2) to GND with the internal pull-up. Door closed: magnet near, reed closed, pin LOW. Door open: reed opens, pin goes HIGH. LED on PB0 (pin 8) through 220 Ω.

void setup()
{
  pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP);       // reed switch
  pinMode(8, OUTPUT);             // alarm LED / buzzer
}
void loop()
{
  if (digitalRead(2) == HIGH)     // door opened
    digitalWrite(8, HIGH);        // latch on
}                                 // reset button clears it
#include <avr/io.h>
int main(void)
{
  DDRB  |=  (1<<PB0);
  DDRD  &= ~(1<<PD2);
  PORTD |=  (1<<PD2);            // pull-up
  while (1)
    if (PIND & (1<<PD2))         // HIGH = open
      PORTB |= (1<<PB0);         // alarm on
}

B. Fan that switches on when it is hot

Spec: LM35 on A0; a 6–9 V DC motor turns on at 30 °C and off at 28 °C. Design: motor powered from the 9 V battery through a 2N7000 (or BC547 if the motor draws under 100 mA) on PB1 (pin 9), flyback diode across the motor, 10 kΩ gate pull-down, grounds joined. Two thresholds (hysteresis, Lesson 5) stop it chattering at 29 °C. Pin 9 is also a PWM pin, so Lesson 8 can later control the speed (Exercise 6).

void setup() { pinMode(9, OUTPUT); }
void loop()
{
  float t = analogRead(A0) * 5.0 / 1024 * 100;   // LM35: degrees C
  if (t >= 30)      digitalWrite(9, HIGH);       // hot: fan on
  else if (t <= 28) digitalWrite(9, LOW);        // cool: fan off
  delay(500);
}

11. Build example: a motor driven by a BC547

This is component test 09 in the Component Tests folder (wiring and pictures there). Use the Lesson 3 section P method. What is new for actuators:

  1. Two supplies: the motor runs from the 9 V battery, never from the Uno 5 V pin. Join the battery − to the Uno GND rail. Never connect the battery + to the Uno 5 V or the breadboard + rail used by the Uno.
  2. Order of wiring: transistor first (check C, B, E), then 1 kΩ from the pin to the base, emitter to GND, motor between battery + and collector, diode across the motor (stripe to battery +). Connect the battery last, after checking.
  3. Test in steps: blink sketch on the pin with an LED in place of the motor first (proves the transistor), then the motor.
  4. Signs of a fault: Uno resets when the motor starts (motor on the Uno supply); motor always on (C and E swapped or transistor damaged); transistor hot (diode reversed, or motor current above 100 mA).

L2. Transducers: the idea

  • A transducer converts energy from one form to another, in either direction. Inward (other energy → electrical) = sensor: LM35 (thermal), LDR (light), potentiometer (rotation). Outward (electrical → other) = actuator: LED (light), motor (motion), speaker or buzzer (sound).
  • Transducers sit at the boundary of the embedded system.
  • A digital sensor's two voltages are not exactly 0 and 5 V: roughly 0–0.8 V reads LOW, 2–5 V reads HIGH. The pushbutton you already used is a digital sensor.
  • If you understand these common sensors and how to connect them, you can connect any other sensor the same way.

L3. Digital sensors: points from class

SensorWhat he added
Reed switch (~Rs 25)Contacts sealed in a glass tube filled with inert gas. It only detects movement across its field of view: someone walking straight towards it is not seen, so mount it where people will cross in front of it. L5. Actuators: the numbers he used

  • An ATmega328P pin gives 5 V and at most 40 mA. If a load tries to draw more, the pin heats up and burns out: "the motor doesn't care what the pin can deliver".
  • Kit motor (6–9 V): about 75 mA with no load, more with a fan or wheel attached. Too much for a pin.
  • Solenoid: straight-line motion (a plunger), about 12 V and 500–600 mA. Typical use: an electric (even IoT) door lock.
  • Thermoelectric / Peltier (named after Jean Charles Peltier): current moves heat from one face to the other, with about 70 °C between the faces. Keep the hot side at 70 °C with a heat sink and fan, and the cold side reaches about 0 °C. A 2×2-inch module needs 12 V 5 A = 60 W (~Rs 1500).
    Pros: no moving parts, no compressed gas, long life, no maintenance, one device for both heating and cooling.
    Cons: high cost per unit of cooling, and poor power efficiency compared with a compressor fridge.
    Uses: cooling overclocked CPUs and the IR sensor of heat-seeking missiles.
  • Incandescent bulb: the filament gives light and heat. 12 V or 230 V and up to amps, so it cannot go on a pin.
  • RGB LED: the longest leg is the common cathode. With it on the left, the others are R, G, B. Treat it like three LEDs, each with its own resistor. Mixing the three gives almost any colour.

L6. The BJT as he taught it

  • Bipolar junction transistor: it has two P–N junctions. It is current-driven: a tiny base–emitter current (µA) lets a much larger collector–emitter current (mA) flow. In his demo the collector current was about 1000× the base current. More base current gives more collector current; zero base current turns it off. So it is an electronic switch, a small current controlling a large one (his water-gate analogy).
  • NPN = P sandwiched between two N layers. You push current base → emitter, and the large current flows collector → emitter. PNP = N between two P layers; current flows emitter → base and emitter → collector. The arrow on the emitter shows the current direction.
  • BC547 datasheet: pin 1 C, pin 2 B, pin 3 E; collector current max 100 mA; up to 45 V between collector and emitter. Very cheap. Big power transistors handle amps and have a hole for bolting to an aluminium heat sink. Small ones need no heat sink.
  • Two separate circuits: the pin supplies only the tiny base current (through the 1 kΩ); the motor's current flows in the collector–emitter circuit from the 9 V battery, whose − terminal is joined to the Uno GND (common ground). The pin never carries the motor current.
  • How to choose a transistor: check two datasheet numbers against your load, the continuous collector (or drain) current and the maximum voltage. The kit motor (75 mA, 9 V) is fine on a BC547 (100 mA, 45 V). Don't buy a far bigger transistor than needed, as it just costs more.

L7. Flyback diode, in his words

When the transistor turns off, the motor keeps spinning for a moment from momentum. A coil spinning in a magnetic field acts as a dynamo, and the collapsing field (self-induction) produces a high-voltage spike in the opposite direction. Through the transistor, that spike destroys it. A diode across the motor, anode to the collector side, cathode to +V, gives the spike a safe path to "fly back". Connect it the other way and there is no protection. It must handle the current and voltage: use a 1N4001 (1 A, 50 V), not a small signal diode. Same effect as the choke in a fluorescent tube light, which makes the high voltage that starts the tube when the starter opens. Reading: Web Resources/06 - Applications of Diodes.md.

L8. MOSFET as he taught it

  • Metal Oxide Semiconductor Field Effect Transistor. Pins: Gate, Drain, Source. Voltage-driven: the gate takes a voltage, not a current, "pressure, not flow" in his water analogy.
  • Why modern designs prefer MOSFETs: much faster switching and less heat (important in small wearable and portable devices). Downside: more expensive (2N7000 about Rs 10–15 vs BC547 a few rupees).
  • N-channel: a positive gate voltage lets current flow drain → source. P-channel: a negative gate voltage, current flows source → drain.
  • No gate resistor is needed in his circuit: no current flows into the gate, so there is nothing to limit.
  • 2N7000: about 200 mA drain current and 60 V, plenty for the 75 mA / 9 V motor. Still needs the flyback diode.
  • BJTs and MOSFETs look identical (same packages): you can only tell them apart by the part number and datasheet.

L9. Relays, as he taught them

  • A relay is a spring-loaded electromagnetic switch. Current in the coil makes a magnetic field that pulls the contact over. When the current stops, the field collapses and the spring pulls the contact back. Terminals: COM (common), NC (normally closed), NO (normally open, closes when the coil is energised).
  • Used for high voltage and current, e.g. a 230 V AC conveyor motor drawing 5–10 A, switched by a 5 V coil.
  • Even a small 5 V relay coil needs about 70 mA, more than a pin's 40 mA. So drive it with a BC547 + 1 kΩ + 1N4001 flyback diode (the coil is an inductor too).
  • A relay breakout board (~Rs 100–125) already has the relay, transistor, resistor, flyback diode, indicator LED and screw terminals. Still know how the circuit works, in case you have to build it.
  • Rule of thumb: use a relay for 230 V AC loads, a transistor/MOSFET for DC loads.
  • Safety: avoid experimenting with mains (230 V can be fatal) unless you are trained. Practise relays with DC supplies (a battery or wall adapter) instead.
  • Ideas he gave: an auto light (LDR + relay switching a 230 V lamp at nightfall) and an auto fan (LM35: fan on above 30 °C, off below).
