1. The world of data (slides 3–6)

Almost everything we do creates digital data: documents and files, media (photos, videos, music), communications (email, chat, video calls), personal data (calendar, contacts, location), health data (wearables, medical records) and financial data (banking, transactions). Scale on the slides: 2.5 quintillion bytes created every day, 5B+ Internet users (2024), 500M+ tweets a day, 720K hours of YouTube video uploaded a day.

Big data (definition) Large pools of data that can be captured, communicated, aggregated, stored and analysed to deliver insights and value.
The 5 V'sMeaningSlide example
VolumeThe sheer amount of data1.2 ZB (2010) → 120+ ZB (2024). 1 ZB (zettabyte) = 1021 bytes
VelocityThe speed at which data arrives and must be processedReal-time streaming and processing
VarietyThe different forms of dataStructured (tables), semi-structured (JSON, XML), unstructured (images, video, text)
VeracityHow trustworthy/accurate the data isData quality and accuracy
ValueThe useful result we get from itBusiness insights and decisions

What we do with data (slide 6): store (databases, file systems, object storage), secure (encryption, access control, compliance), share (APIs, collaboration platforms), process (real-time analytics, batch processing), analyze (ML, AI, BI dashboards), stream (IoT sensors, live pipelines). A single company cannot cheaply buy enough machines for all this — this is what pushes computing into the cloud.

3. What is cloud computing? (slides 9–12)

Definition (slide 9) "Cloud computing is the delivery of computing as a service rather than a product, whereby shared resources, software and information are provided to computers and other devices as a metered service over a network."
Short form: on-demand IT services on a pay-as-you-go basis over the Internet.

Key concepts (slide 10):

  • Remotely hosted: services and data are stored and run on remote infrastructure, not on local machines.
  • Ubiquitous access: accessible from anywhere, anytime, on any device.
  • Commodified computing: computing as a utility like water or electricity; pay only for what you consume.
  • Multi-tenancy: multiple users (tenants) share the same physical infrastructure securely and independently, like flats in one apartment block.

NIST's five essential characteristics (slide 11) — very likely exam question

NIST (the US National Institute of Standards and Technology) wrote the standard definition. A service is "cloud" only if it has all five.

#CharacteristicMeaningExample
1On-demand self-serviceUsers provision computing resources automatically, without human interaction from the provider.You click "Launch instance" and get a server in a minute; nobody at AWS approves it.
2Broad network accessAvailable over the network through standard mechanisms (phones, tablets, laptops).Open Google Drive from a phone or a laptop browser.
3Resource poolingThe provider's resources are pooled to serve many consumers using a multi-tenant model; the customer does not know the exact physical location.Your VM and another company's VM run on the same physical server.
4Rapid elasticityCapabilities can be provisioned and released quickly, even automatically, to scale with demand.An online shop goes from 2 to 20 servers during a sale, then back to 2.
5Measured serviceResource use is monitored, controlled and reported — pay for what you use.Bill = CPU-hours + GB stored + GB transferred.

Memory aid: "On Broad Pools, Rapid Meters" (On-demand, Broad, Pooling, Rapid, Measured).

Why cloud computing? (slide 12)

BenefitExplanation
Pay-as-you-goNo upfront capital expenditure (CapEx) on hardware; costs become operating expenditure (OpEx). Reduces Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) = all costs of owning (hardware, power, cooling, staff, space).
Scale instantlyRent and release resources as needed.
Simplified ITThe provider manages the infrastructure; you need only Internet access.

Resource efficiencySharing reduces idle resources; better CPU/storage utilisation.

Worked example: own server vs pay-as-you-go (illustrative numbers)

A student project needs a server for a 2-week exam-results peak each semester (2 semesters a year), and almost nothing the rest of the year.

OptionCalculationYearly cost
Buy a server (sized for the peak)Rs. 600,000 hardware spread over 3 years = 200,000/yr, + power/maintenance 50,000/yrRs. 250,000, mostly idle
Cloud VM, pay per hour4 weeks × 7 days × 24 h = 672 h × Rs. 100/hRs. 67,200, nothing paid when idle

The point (not the exact numbers): for spiky or uncertain demand, measured service plus elasticity is much cheaper than owning capacity for the peak.

4. Cloud service models (slides 13–17)

A service model says how much of the computing stack the provider manages for you. The stack (bottom to top): networking, storage, servers, virtualization, operating system (OS), middleware, runtime, data, applications.

  • Middleware: software between the OS and applications (web servers, message queues, database connectors).
  • Runtime: the environment the code runs in (Java JVM, Python interpreter, Node.js).
Figure 2 — Who manages what. Moving right, the provider takes over more layers and you control less (slide 14 shows the IaaS split).

PaaS features (slide 15): auto-scaling (handles traffic spikes), CI/CD pipelines (automated build, test, deploy), managed databases (no DBA; provider handles backups), dev frameworks (pre-built runtimes), built-in networking (load balancers, SSL certificates), monitoring (built-in logging and performance tools).

SaaS examples (slide 16): email (Gmail, Outlook 365), video conferencing (Zoom, Teams, Meet), CRM (Salesforce, HubSpot), office suites (Microsoft 365, Google Workspace), media (Spotify, Netflix), security (Okta, CrowdStrike), analytics (Tableau Online, Power BI), collaboration (Slack, Notion, Asana).

5. Cloud deployment models (slide 18)

A deployment model says who owns the infrastructure and who can use it (a different question from the service model).

Figure 3 — Deployment models. Community cloud is named in Lecture 01 (slide 7); Lecture 02 adds multi-cloud.
ModelDefinitionProsConsExamples

Hybrid vs multi-cloud (common confusion): hybrid = private + public; multi-cloud = more than one public provider. An organization can be both.

6. Enabling technologies and characteristics (slides 19–21)

Internet & Web 2.0High-bandwidth, always-on connectivity makes remote computing feasible.

Figure 4 — Virtualization stack (slide 20).

Virtualization: the core enabler. A hypervisor is software that sits on the hardware and creates virtual machines (VMs), each acting like a separate computer with its own OS. Benefits on the slide:

  • Server consolidation: many lightly-used servers become VMs on fewer physical machines.
  • Isolation between workloads: a crash in one VM does not affect others.
  • Rapid VM provisioning: minutes, not weeks.
  • Snapshotting & migration: save a VM's state; move it to another host.

Details: Lessons 5 and 8.

Cloud computing characteristics (slide 21): massive scale (global data centres, millions of servers); homogeneity (standardised hardware for consistent management); resilient computing (auto-healing recovery); geographic distribution (regions for low latency and compliance); low-cost software (open-source stacks); advanced security (shared responsibility model); service orientation (everything exposed as an API — composable, interoperable); virtualization (physical resources abstracted into flexible logical ones).

Market (slides 22–23): AWS 31%, Azure 25%, Google Cloud 11%, Alibaba 4% (Synergy Research, Q3 2024). Public cloud revenue grew from $242B (2019) to an estimated $679B (2024) — about 3× in 5 years — and is expected to exceed $1 trillion by 2027.

7. Modern cloud developments (2020–2025): overview (slide 24)

Six trends: containers & Kubernetes · serverless computing · edge & fog computing · AI/ML as a service · Zero Trust security · sustainability & green cloud.

7.1 Containers and Docker (slide 25)

Figure 5 — VMs vs containers (slide 25). Containers are faster, lighter and more portable than VMs.

A container packages an application with its libraries and settings, but not a whole OS: all containers on a host share the host's OS kernel (the core of the OS that controls hardware). Docker is the most popular tool to build and run containers. Lesson 3 (Docker) and Lesson 5 (VM vs container) go deeper.

7.2 Kubernetes: container orchestration (slide 26)

With hundreds of containers on many machines, someone must decide where each runs, restart failed ones and spread traffic. Kubernetes (K8s) automates the deployment, scaling and management of containerized applications. Six features:

FeatureWhat it does
Auto-scalingScales Pods (groups of containers) up/down based on CPU, memory or custom metrics
Self-healingRestarts failed containers; kills containers that fail health checks
Load balancingDistributes network traffic to Pods for a stable deployment
Rolling updatesDeploys new versions with zero downtime; instant rollback
Service discoveryAutomatically assigns DNS names and IP addresses to containers
Storage orchestrationAutomatically mounts storage — local, cloud or network

Managed Kubernetes: Amazon EKS, Azure AKS, Google GKE. Full detail in Lesson 6.

7.3 Serverless computing (FaaS) (slide 27)

Key sentence"Serverless" does not mean no servers — it means developers don't manage servers. The provider provisions, scales and manages infrastructure automatically.
Figure 6 — Serverless flow (slide 27).
Advantages
  • No server management
  • Auto-scales, including to zero
  • Pay only per invocation
  • Fast deployment
Disadvantages
  • Cold start latency: the first call after idle is slow because the provider must start a new environment
  • Debugging is harder (short-lived, distributed functions)

Examples: AWS Lambda, Azure Functions, Google Cloud Functions, Cloudflare Workers. Serverless vs containers (knowledge check): serverless for event-driven, infrequent, short-lived functions; containers for long-running, stateful, complex apps.

7.4 Edge, fog and cloud computing (slides 28–29)

Edge computing = processing data close to where it is generated (at the "edge" of the network) instead of in a central data centre. Reason: latency — sending data to a far data centre and waiting for the answer takes too long for some uses.

Figure 7 — Edge → fog → cloud tiers (slides 28–29).
AspectCloud computingFog computingEdge computing

LatencyHigh (50–150 ms)Medium (10–50 ms)Ultra-low (<10 ms)

Edge use cases: autonomous vehicles (real-time decisions cannot wait for a cloud round-trip), industrial IoT (sensors control machines locally), remote healthcare (devices process vitals for instant alerts), cloud gaming (ultra-low latency). MEC (in the slide title) = Multi-access Edge Computing: edge servers placed in the mobile/telecom network.

7.5 AI and machine learning in the cloud (slides 30–31)

Training large AI models needs thousands of GPUs, which only the cloud can supply at that scale: "the cloud is the engine of the AI revolution". Platforms: AWS SageMaker (build, train, deploy), Azure AI / OpenAI (GPT-4, DALL-E, Cognitive Services), Google Vertex AI (AutoML, model garden, Gemini); NVIDIA A100/H100 GPU instances. Generative AI cloud spending is projected to exceed $200B a year by 2028.

GenAI and cloud are symbiotic (each helps the other): foundation models (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, LLaMA) need cloud GPUs; providers built AI chips (TPUs, Trainium, Inferentia); AI is offered through simple REST APIs; companies fine-tune models on private data; VPCs and private endpoints protect sensitive training data; new vector databases (Pinecone, Weaviate, pgvector) power AI apps.

8. Cloud security (slides 32–33)

8.1 The shared responsibility model

Figure 8 — Shared responsibility (slide 32). The provider secures the cloud itself; the customer secures what they put in it.

Example: if a company leaves an S3 bucket open to the public, that is the customer's fault (configuration "in" the cloud), not AWS's. IAM = Identity and Access Management: users, roles and permissions.

8.2 Zero Trust security

Traditional security trusted everything inside the company network (a "castle and moat" perimeter). In the cloud there is no clear perimeter, so this is obsolete. Zero Trust: "never trust, always verify" — assume a breach and verify continuously.

PrincipleMeaning
Verify explicitlyAuthenticate and authorize using all data points: identity, location, device health, workload
Least privilegeGive only the access needed, at the right time, for the right purpose
Assume breachDesign as if attackers are already inside; minimise the blast radius (how much damage one breach can do) and segment access

Tools: Microsoft Entra ID, Zscaler, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks SASE.

9. Cost, compliance, sustainability, strategy (slides 34–38)

9.1 FinOps: cloud cost optimisation

FinOps = bringing financial accountability to cloud spending (costs can spiral without management).

TechniqueMeaning
Right-sizingMatch instance size to the real workload; oversized instances waste money
Reserved instancesCommit for 1–3 years for predictable workloads; save up to 72%
Spot / preemptible VMsUse spare capacity at 60–90% discount, for fault-tolerant work (the provider can take it back)
Auto-scalingScale down at low demand; don't pay for idle resources overnight/weekends
Storage tieringMove rarely used data to cheaper tiers (Glacier, Archive)
Cost allocation tagsTag every resource to track spending by team, project or environment

Worked example: a VM costs $100/month on demand. Reserved (up to 72% off) ≈ $28/month; spot (60–90% off) ≈ $10–40/month but can be interrupted. A web server that must always run → reserved; a batch video-rendering job that can restart → spot.

Data sovereignty = data is subject to the laws of the country where it is stored, so some data must stay in-country.

9.3 Sustainability and green cloud (slide 36)

Data centres use about 1–2% of global electricity. Google: carbon neutral since 2007, targeting 24/7 carbon-free energy by 2030. Microsoft: carbon negative and water positive by 2030, 100% renewable. AWS: 100% renewable by 2025, net-zero carbon by 2040. Shared cloud infrastructure is 65–80% more energy-efficient than individual on-premises servers.

9.4 Multi-cloud and hybrid strategy (slide 37)

87% of enterprises use several providers. Five reasons: (1) avoid vendor lock-in (not dependent on one provider's pricing, features or outages); (2) best-of-breed (AWS for compute, GCP for AI/ML, Azure for Microsoft integration); (3) regulatory compliance (local provider for in-country data); (4) cost arbitrage (use the cheapest per workload); (5) redundancy (fail over to another provider during an outage). Tools: Terraform, Pulumi, Kubernetes (cloud-agnostic), HashiCorp Vault.

Cloud-native apps are designed from the ground up to exploit the cloud.

12. Exam-style questions with model answers

Q1. Define cloud computing and list the five essential characteristics defined by NIST. (6 marks)
Cloud computing is the delivery of computing as a service rather than a product, where shared resources, software and information are provided to devices as a metered service over a network (on-demand IT on a pay-as-you-go basis). NIST characteristics: on-demand self-service (provision without human interaction), broad network access (standard devices over the network), resource pooling (multi-tenant shared resources), rapid elasticity (scale out and in quickly), measured service (usage monitored and billed).

Q2. Compare IaaS, PaaS and SaaS in terms of what the customer manages, with one example each. (6)
IaaS: provider manages networking, storage, servers and virtualization; the customer manages the OS, middleware, runtime, data and applications (AWS EC2). PaaS: the provider also manages OS, middleware and runtime; the customer manages only the application and data (Google App Engine). SaaS: the provider manages the whole stack; the customer only uses the software (Gmail, Microsoft 365).

Q3. Distinguish public, private and hybrid clouds and state one advantage of each. (6)
Public: owned by a third party and shared over the Internet — low cost and high scalability. Private: operated exclusively for one organization — high security, control and compliance. Hybrid: public and private bound together with data/app portability — flexibility and cost optimisation (keep sensitive data private, burst peaks to public).

Q4. Explain the shared responsibility model with an example. (4)
Security is split: the provider is responsible for security OF the cloud (physical infrastructure, network hardware, hypervisor); the customer is responsible for security IN the cloud (data encryption, IAM, network configuration, application security). E.g. if a customer misconfigures an S3 bucket as public and data leaks, it is the customer's responsibility.

Q5. "Serverless means there are no servers." Discuss. Give two advantages and two disadvantages of serverless computing. (5)
False: servers still exist, but the provider provisions, scales and manages them, so developers do not. Advantages: no server management; auto-scales to zero and pay only per invocation (also fast deployment). Disadvantages: cold-start latency; harder debugging.

Q6. Differentiate between a virtual machine and a container. (4)
A VM virtualizes the hardware and runs a full guest OS on a hypervisor — strong isolation, but GBs in size and minutes to boot. A container packages the app and dependencies and shares the host OS kernel through a container runtime — lighter (MBs), starts in seconds, more portable, weaker isolation.

Q7. Compare edge, fog and cloud computing. Why does an autonomous vehicle need edge computing? (6)
Cloud: centralized data centres, high latency (50–150 ms), massive compute, batch analytics. Fog: between edge and cloud, medium latency (10–50 ms), aggregates edge data. Edge: on device/gateway, ultra-low latency (<10 ms), lightweight real-time compute at the source. A vehicle must brake within milliseconds; waiting for a round-trip to a far data centre is too slow and the connection may drop, so decisions are made at the edge.

Q8. State three principles of Zero Trust security. (3)
Verify explicitly (authenticate using identity, location, device health, workload); least privilege (minimum access only when needed); assume breach (design as if attackers are inside, minimise blast radius, segment access).

Q9. Explain why virtualization is called the core enabler of cloud computing. (4)
A hypervisor divides one physical server into many isolated VMs. This allows resource pooling and multi-tenancy (many customers on shared hardware), high utilisation (server consolidation), rapid provisioning (on-demand self-service, elasticity), and snapshotting/migration for resilience — all the properties the cloud depends on.

Q10. Give four FinOps techniques for reducing cloud costs. (4)
Right-sizing instances; reserved instances for predictable workloads (up to 72% saving); spot/preemptible VMs for fault-tolerant work (60–90% discount); auto-scaling down when idle; storage tiering; cost-allocation tags.

Q11. Why do 87% of enterprises use a multi-cloud strategy? (5)
To avoid vendor lock-in; to use best-of-breed services from each provider; for regulatory compliance (in-country providers); for cost arbitrage between providers; and for redundancy (fail over during one provider's outage).

Q12. Explain the 5 V's of big data. (5)
Volume (amount: 120+ ZB in 2024), Velocity (speed of arrival, real-time streams), Variety (structured, semi-structured, unstructured), Veracity (quality and accuracy), Value (insights and business decisions).

W7. Compare edge, fog and cloud computing. Why does an autonomous vehicle need edge computing? (6)

Cloud: centralized data centres, high latency (50–150 ms), massive compute, batch analytics. Fog: between edge and cloud, medium latency (10–50 ms), aggregates edge data. Edge: on device/gateway, ultra-low latency (<10 ms), lightweight real-time compute at the source. A vehicle must brake within milliseconds; waiting for a round-trip to a far data centre is too slow and the connection may drop, so decisions are made at the edge.

W8. State three principles of Zero Trust security. (3)

Verify explicitly (authenticate using identity, location, device health, workload); least privilege (minimum access only when needed); assume breach (design as if attackers are inside, minimise blast radius, segment access).

W9. Explain why virtualization is called the core enabler of cloud computing. (4)

A hypervisor divides one physical server into many isolated VMs. This allows resource pooling and multi-tenancy (many customers on shared hardware), high utilisation (server consolidation), rapid provisioning (on-demand self-service, elasticity), and snapshotting/migration for resilience — all the properties the cloud depends on.

W10. Give four FinOps techniques for reducing cloud costs. (4)

Right-sizing instances; reserved instances for predictable workloads (up to 72% saving); spot/preemptible VMs for fault-tolerant work (60–90% discount); auto-scaling down when idle; storage tiering; cost-allocation tags.

W11. Why do 87% of enterprises use a multi-cloud strategy? (5)

To avoid vendor lock-in; to use best-of-breed services from each provider; for regulatory compliance (in-country providers); for cost arbitrage between providers; and for redundancy (fail over during one provider's outage).

W12. Explain the 5 V's of big data. (5)

Volume (amount: 120+ ZB in 2024), Velocity (speed of arrival, real-time streams), Variety (structured, semi-structured, unstructured), Veracity (quality and accuracy), Value (insights and business decisions).

Hybrid = multi-cloudHybrid = private + public. Multi-cloud = several public providers.
"Serverless has no servers."Servers exist; you just don't manage them.

"The provider is responsible for all security in the cloud."Shared responsibility: the customer secures data, IAM, network config, apps.
