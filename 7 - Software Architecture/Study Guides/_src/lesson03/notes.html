1. Problem space and solution space

Software work moves from understanding a problem to building a solution (the SDLC). The lecture's Venn diagram shows three regions: problem framing, solution oriented, and where they overlap, systems thinking + design thinking. In other words, before jumping to a solution you must frame the problem properly, and both thinking styles help you move between the two spaces.

2. Traditional thinking vs systems thinking

Traditional thinking looks at a simple one-way link between a cause and an effect. Systems thinking asks us to look beyond surface-level "fixes" toward the network of underlying causes and effects.

The iceberg model

LevelQuestion
EventsWhat happened?
PatternsWhat is happening over time?
StructuresWhat is enabling this pattern to continue?
Mental modelsIn what ways have our mental models created or sustained the structures in place?

You analyse and understand by working down the iceberg (from visible events to hidden beliefs) and you innovate and transform by working back up. In the lecture's second Venn diagram, systems thinking is about what was? (the past), design thinking about what can be? (the future) and where they overlap is what is? (the present).

Software example (illustrative): event = the site crashed on sale day; pattern = it crashes every sale; structure = one database and no caching; mental model = "traffic will stay small, we will optimise later". Fixing the event alone (restarting the server) is a surface fix.

3. Design thinking beyond digital

The lecture frames design thinking as going beyond digital products to radical innovation: "keep up the design thinking mindset and start hunting for the next big opportunity". It is a human-centred way of finding and solving problems, built on a set of mindsets (next section) and a process of exploring and then focusing (section 5).

The right team for new ideas

New ideas are developed by people with great energy, tireless commitment and heads filled with visions. To succeed we usually need: a (customer) need; an interdisciplinary team; the right mindset; the necessary leeway for experimentation; creativity; and the courage to question what exists.

5. Divergent and convergent thinking

  • Divergent thinking opens up: identify, discuss, collect known options and different perspectives.
  • Convergent thinking narrows down: prioritise, select, identify actions, refine and consolidate.
  • Between them lies the groan zone: the uncomfortable, messy middle where many ideas are on the table. Teams must be aware of it and be facilitated through it to a point of decision.

The double diamond: think like a system, act like an entrepreneur

DiamondPhaseActivity
1: the problem (think like a system)Discover (diverge)Behaviour-led design research
Define (converge)Workshops and idea generation, ending in the problem definition
2: the solution (act like an entrepreneur)Develop (diverge)Review ideas through culture and design thinking
Deliver (converge)Prototyping, selection and mentoring

Link to architecture: the first diamond is problem space (requirements, stakeholders, quality attributes); the second is solution space (components, patterns, prototypes). Lesson 4 turns this into a method for going from problem domain to system design.

Key takeaways Systems thinking looks at the network of causes and effects (iceberg: events, patterns, structures, mental models). Design thinking is human-centred, built on mindsets such as curiosity, empathy, iteration and co-creation. Diverge to open up, converge to decide, expect the groan zone in between. Double diamond: Discover, Define (problem), Develop, Deliver (solution). Teams need a customer need, interdisciplinary people, the right mindset, room to experiment and courage to question.
